## Text hearts and heart emojis are not the same thing

A heart you copy from this page is one of two kinds, and it helps to know which one you are pasting.

**Text hearts** such as ♡, ♥, ❥, ❣ and ღ are ordinary characters, like letters. They have no color of their own. They take the color, size and weight of the text around them, which is why they look clean in a minimal bio and match whatever font a designer chose. ♡ and ♥ come from the playing-card suits, ❥ and ❦ are decorative dingbats, and ღ is actually a letter of the Georgian alphabet that people borrowed because it looks like a hand-drawn heart.

**Heart emojis** such as ❤️, 💜 and 💖 are drawn as small color pictures by each platform. Apple, Google, Samsung and Microsoft each have their own artwork, so the same heart looks a little different on an iPhone and on an Android phone, but it stays the same color everywhere.

The red heart sits between the two. The plain character ❤ existed as a text ornament long before emoji, and the colored ❤️ is the same character followed by an invisible marker that asks for the emoji style. Without that marker, a device can show ❤ either as a flat text heart or as a red emoji, depending on the app and font. If you want a guaranteed red heart, copy ❤️ from the color set. If you want a heart that matches your text color, copy ❤ or ♥ from the text set. The combo ❤︎ ❤︎ uses the opposite marker, which asks for the text style.

## Hearts that move, sparkle and break

The feelings set covers hearts that do more than sit there. 💓 beats, 💗 grows, 💞 revolves, 💖 sparkles and 💘 has Cupid's arrow through it. 💝 is wrapped with a ribbon like a gift, and 💟 is a heart inside a frame that some people use as a decorative bullet. ❣️ is the heart used as an exclamation mark, which works well at the end of a happy sentence.

Two newer hearts carry a story. ❤️‍🩹, the mending heart, is a bandaged heart for recovery, healing after a breakup or getting better after an illness. ❤️‍🔥 is the heart on fire for passion. Both are built from several characters joined together, so on an older device they can split into a red heart followed by a separate bandage or flame.

## How to type a heart without copy and paste

Copying is the quickest way, but every major device can type hearts directly.

- **iPhone and Android:** open the emoji keyboard, tap the search field and type "heart." The results include every color. Some keyboards also suggest a heart when you type the word "love."
- **Windows:** press the Windows key and the period key together to open the emoji panel, then type "heart." The panel also has a symbols tab with text characters. In many Windows apps with a numeric keypad, holding Alt and typing 3 on the keypad produces ♥.
- **Mac:** press Control, Command and Space to open the Character Viewer, then search "heart." It shows both emoji hearts and text hearts such as ♡ and ❥.
- **Some apps:** a few messaging and chat apps turn the classic `<3` into a heart emoji when you send it. Others leave it as typed. If you want the old-school text version to stay put, copy `<3` from the combos set.

## Hearts in bios, usernames and captions

Hearts are one of the most common decorations in social media bios. A few practical points make them look intentional rather than cluttered:

- **One style per bio.** Mixing text hearts with color emoji hearts often looks busy. Pick ♡ for a soft, minimal bio or a single emoji color that matches your theme.
- **Use borders sparingly.** A divider like ─── ♡ ─── can separate a bio into lines, but on a narrow phone screen a long border wraps onto two lines.
- **Check usernames.** Many games and platforms allow only letters, numbers and a few punctuation marks in usernames, so hearts may be rejected or stripped. Display names are usually more flexible than handles.
- **Test the newest hearts.** 🩵, 🩶 and 🩷 are recent additions. If many of your readers use older phones, a classic ❤️ or ♡ is safer.

## Accessibility: how screen readers hear hearts

People who use screen readers hear every symbol read aloud by its name. A single ❤️ is read as something like "red heart," which is short and clear. A text heart may be read by its formal name, such as "white heart suit" for ♡, which can sound odd in the middle of a sentence. Ornaments like ❦ and ❥ may be read with long names or skipped, depending on the screen reader.

Rows of hearts are the real problem. A border of ten hearts becomes the same name read ten times, and a username full of symbols can be hard to follow at all. A few habits help:

- Put hearts at the end of a sentence or caption, not between words.
- Use one heart to make a point and save rows of hearts for places where they are clearly decoration.
- Keep important words, such as your name or a call to action, free of symbols so they are read correctly.
