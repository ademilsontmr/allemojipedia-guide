## What kaomoji are

Kaomoji are faces made from text characters. The word is Japanese: *kao* means face and *moji* means character. Where a Western emoticon such as :) or ;-) is read by tilting your head to the left, a kaomoji is read upright, the way you would look at a real face: (^_^) is a smile, (T_T) is crying, and (°o°) is surprise.

That upright layout changes what carries the feeling. In a sideways emoticon the mouth does most of the work, which is why :) and :( differ only in the mouth. In a kaomoji the eyes usually decide the mood. Curved eyes like ^ ^ read as happy, T T or ╥ ╥ read as tears streaming down, and wide eyes like ⊙ ⊙ read as shock. Parentheses act as the outline of the head, and anything outside them becomes arms, props or motion.

Kaomoji are not the same thing as emoji. An emoji such as 😊 is a single character that your phone or computer draws as a small picture in its own style. A kaomoji is several ordinary characters in a row, so it looks like plain text and takes on the font around it. If you want picture emoji instead, the [emoji copy and paste page](/emoji-copy-and-paste/) has the most used ones in one place.

## Where kaomoji come from

Kaomoji grew out of Japanese online culture, in text chat, bulletin boards and early mobile messaging, before picture emoji were common. Japanese keyboards and fonts gave people easy access to a much larger set of characters than a basic English keyboard: full-width letters, katakana such as ツ and ノ, box-drawing lines and many shapes. Writers combined them into faces, and a few, like the shrug ¯\\\_(ツ)\_/¯ and the table flip (╯°□°)╯︵ ┻━┻, spread into English-speaking forums, gaming chat and social media, where they are now used by people who have never typed Japanese.

The style kept evolving after emoji arrived. Kaomoji are still popular because they can show things a single emoji cannot: a whole body flipping a table, a bear giving a hug, a tiny person running away. For the longer story of how text faces led to picture emoji, see [the history of emoji](/guides/history-of-emoji/).

## How to read a kaomoji

Most kaomoji follow the same building blocks. Once you know them, you can read faces you have never seen and make your own.

| Part | Common characters | What it signals | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eyes | ^ ^, ◕ ◕, ≧ ≦ | Happy, smiling, excited | (≧▽≦) |
| Eyes | T T, ╥ ╥, ಥ ಥ | Crying, tears | (╥﹏╥) |
| Eyes | ⊙ ⊙, ° °, O O | Surprise, shock | (⊙_⊙) |
| Eyes | ¬ ¬, ಠ ಠ | Suspicion, disapproval | ಠ_ಠ |
| Mouth | ▽, ‿, ω | Open smile, gentle smile, cat-like mouth | (^ω^) |
| Mouth | Д, 益 | Shouting, gritted teeth | (｀Д´) |
| Cheeks | * or 〃 beside the eyes, ⁄ ⁄ | Blushing, shyness | (〃▽〃) |
| Sweat | ; after the face | Nervous, awkward | (^_^;) |
| Arms | ノ, ﾉ, ╯ ╯, ＼ ／ | Waving, cheering, throwing | ＼(^o^)／ |
| Extras | ♡, ☆, zzZ, ε=ε= | Love, a wink of sparkle, sleep, running | (－_－) zzZ |

A few faces have meanings that are worth knowing before you send them. The look of disapproval ಠ_ಠ is a flat stare, not a happy face. The lenny face ( ͡° ͜ʖ ͡°) is sly and mischievous and is often read as suggestive, so it fits joking among friends better than a work chat. A table flip says "I give up" or "this is ridiculous" in a mostly playful way, and ┬─┬ノ( º _ ºノ), putting the table back, is the calm reply. For how picture emoji pick up similar second meanings, see [emoji meanings in texting](/guides/emoji-meanings-in-texting/).

If you are choosing between a text face and a picture, these pairs say roughly the same thing. Kaomoji tend to feel softer and more playful, while emoji are quicker to read at a glance.

## How to type kaomoji without copy and paste

Copying from this page is the fastest route, but several devices have kaomoji built in.

- **Windows 10 and 11:** press the Windows key and the period key together to open the emoji panel. One of its tabs holds kaomoji, grouped by mood. Click a face to insert it.
- **Chromebook:** the emoji picker includes an emoticons section next to emoji and symbols. The [Chromebook emoji guide](/guides/how-to-do-emojis-on-chromebook/) shows how to open it.
- **iPhone and Android:** add the Japanese keyboard in your keyboard settings. It includes a list of ready-made faces, so you can switch to it, pick a face and switch back.
- **Text replacement:** on iPhone, go to Settings, General, Keyboard, Text Replacement and save a face with a short shortcut such as *shrug*. On a Mac, the same feature is under Keyboard settings as Text Replacements. Many Android keyboards offer something similar; Gboard calls it the personal dictionary.

The [guide to typing emojis on any device](/guides/how-to-type-emojis-on-any-device/) covers the emoji side of each of these keyboards, and the [Mac emoji guide](/guides/how-to-get-emojis-on-mac/) explains the Mac character viewer, which is useful for finding single symbols such as ♡ or ☆ to add to your own faces.

## Where kaomoji work and where they break

Kaomoji work almost anywhere you can type text: messages, comments, captions, bios, usernames that allow symbols, and chat in many games. They are plain text, so they copy cleanly and do not depend on an app's emoji set. A few things can still go wrong.

**Missing characters.** If a device's fonts lack one of the characters, it shows a box or a question mark instead. The faces on this page use characters that current iPhone, Android, Windows and Mac fonts display, but very old phones, smart TVs, game consoles and some embedded screens have smaller fonts. Faces with stacked accent marks, like some crying faces, are the most fragile. [Why emojis show as boxes](/guides/why-emojis-show-as-boxes/) explains the cause in detail.

**Fonts change the shape.** Because kaomoji are text, they look slightly different in every font. A face that lines up neatly in one app can look lopsided in another, especially faces that mix full-width and narrow characters.

**Markdown eats backslashes.** Apps that format text with Markdown, Discord among them, treat a backslash as a formatting code, so the shrug loses its left arm and turns into ¯\_(ツ)\_/¯. Type two backslashes in those apps to keep it.

**Length limits and filters.** A kaomoji uses several characters, sometimes more than ten, which counts against short limits on usernames and display names. Some games and sites only allow basic letters and numbers in names and strip everything else.

**Screen readers.** Screen readers handle kaomoji poorly. Depending on settings, they may read each character by name, which turns a cute face into a long string of words like "left parenthesis, circumflex, low line", or skip the symbols entirely so the meaning disappears. If a message matters, say the important part in words and use the kaomoji as decoration. The [screen reader guide](/guides/emoji-accessibility-screen-readers/) has more on writing posts that read well aloud.
