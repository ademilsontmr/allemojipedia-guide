Emojis are standardized as characters, not as meanings. The Unicode Consortium defines that 👍 is "thumbs up"; it does not and cannot define what a raised thumb means to the person receiving it. Hand gestures keep the regional meanings of the physical gestures they depict, a number of emojis carry Japanese meanings that the rest of the world never learned, and colors, numbers and even a plain smile shift by country. This guide lists the differences that are well documented and explains how to write for a mixed audience.

One caution applies throughout. Culture is not uniform within a country, and younger, more online people everywhere tend to share a global set of emoji habits. Treat what follows as "this reading exists and is common enough to matter," not "everyone in this country thinks this."

## Hand gestures that are rude somewhere

A gesture emoji is a picture of a real gesture, and real gestures have regional histories. These are the ones most likely to cause trouble. For the everyday meanings of each, see the guide to [hand gesture emojis](/guides/hand-gesture-emojis-meaning/).

| Emoji | Common meaning in the US and UK | Where it can mean something else |
|---|---|---|
| 👍 Thumbs up | Approval, agreement | Traditionally an insult in Iran, Iraq and parts of West Africa and southern Europe |
| 👌 OK hand | Okay, perfect | Obscene in Brazil and Turkey; "zero, worthless" in France; "money" in Japan |
| 🤘 Sign of the horns | Rock music | In Italy, Spain, Portugal and parts of Latin America, an accusation that someone's partner is unfaithful |
| ✋ Raised hand | Stop, high five, hello | In Greece, an open palm pushed toward someone is a traditional insult called the moutza |
| 🤞 Crossed fingers | Good luck, hoping | In Vietnam, an obscene gesture |
| 🤙 Call me hand | Call me; "hang loose" | The number six in Chinese finger counting; the friendly shaka in Hawaii |
| ✌️ Victory hand | Peace, victory | With the palm facing inward, an insult in the UK, Ireland, Australia and New Zealand |
| 🤌 Pinched fingers | "Italian" emphasis | "What do you want?" in Italy; "wait a moment" in Israel and parts of the Arab world |

Some detail on the most common ones:

**[Thumbs up](/emojis/thumbs-up/).** The approving meaning has spread worldwide through film, television and the Like button, and plenty of people in the Middle East use the emoji exactly as Americans do. The older rude meaning has not vanished, though, and it is not worth the risk in a first message to a client or an older relative there. A written "Sounds good" costs nothing.

**[OK hand](/emojis/ok-hand/).** This is the gesture with the widest spread of meanings. Beyond the regional senses in the table, it picked up an association with white-supremacist trolling in the United States starting around 2017, and the Anti-Defamation League added it to its hate symbols database in 2019 while stressing that most uses remain innocent. Divers also use it to mean "I am fine." Context almost always makes the intent clear, but it is no longer the neutral choice it once was.

**[Sign of the horns](/emojis/sign-of-the-horns/).** Pointed at a person in Italy or Spain, the horns call them a cuckold. Pointed downward in Italy, the same hand shape wards off bad luck. Do not confuse it with the [love-you gesture](/emojis/love-you-gesture/), which adds the thumb and comes from American Sign Language.

**[Victory hand](/emojis/victory-hand/).** Every major vendor draws this emoji palm outward, which is the harmless version, so the emoji is safe in Britain. It is worth knowing because the physical gesture with the back of the hand outward is a real insult there. In Japan, South Korea and much of East Asia, the palm-out V is the standard pose for photos and carries no political meaning.

**[Pinched fingers](/emojis/pinched-fingers/).** Proposed as an Italian gesture, it is used in Israel to ask for patience, in parts of the Arab world similarly, and in India as a gesture for eating or food. Using it to caricature Italians is a joke that wears thin quickly with Italians.

**Pointing.** Pointing at people with the index finger is considered rude in a number of cultures, including Malaysia and Indonesia, where an open hand or thumb is used instead. An emoji such as the [index pointing at the viewer](/emojis/index-pointing-at-the-viewer/) can feel more aggressive than intended.

## Emojis that carry Japanese meanings

Emoji began on Japanese mobile phones in the late 1990s, and the first Unicode emoji set was designed for compatibility with the Japanese carriers. Many pictures therefore encode Japanese gestures, school customs and manga drawing conventions that readers elsewhere have reinterpreted. The background is in the [history of emoji](/guides/history-of-emoji/).

| Emoji | Meaning in its Japanese origin | Common reading elsewhere |
|---|---|---|
| 🙏 Folded hands | Please, thank you, sorry | Prayer; sometimes mistaken for a high five |
| 🙇 Person bowing | Deep apology or a sincere request | Lying down, exhausted, doing push-ups |
| 💁 Person tipping hand | Information desk attendant | Sass, "whatever" |
| 😤 Face with steam from nose | Triumph, pride | Anger, huffing |
| 😪 Sleepy face | Asleep (the bubble is a manga sleep sign) | Sad, a single tear |
| 💢 Anger symbol | Bulging veins of anger in manga | An abstract red shape |
| 💮 White flower | A teacher's "well done" stamp | A decorative flower |
| ⭕ and ❌ | Correct and incorrect | A circle, and "no" or "delete" |
| 💩 Pile of poo | Playful; associated with good luck | A crude joke |

Notes on a few of these:

- [Folded hands](/emojis/folded-hands/) is the best example of an emoji with three legitimate meanings: thanks or request in Japan, the namaste greeting across South Asia, and prayer in the West. All three are friendly, so it rarely offends, but "🙏" after a request reads as "please" to some and "I'm praying for it" to others.
- [Person bowing](/emojis/person-bowing/) depicts a deep, kneeling bow used for serious apology or a big favor. Sending it to a Japanese colleague over a small matter can seem excessive, while Western readers often miss the apology completely.
- The [face with steam from nose](/emojis/face-with-steam-from-nose/) was originally named "face with look of triumph." The steam is a manga sign of a proud snort.
- The [sleepy face](/emojis/sleepy-face/) and [sweat droplets](/emojis/sweat-droplets/) use manga visual shorthand. The same tradition explains the [anger symbol](/emojis/anger-symbol/).
- In Japanese schools a circle marks a right answer and a cross a wrong one. The [hollow red circle](/emojis/hollow-red-circle/) is therefore positive, and the [cross mark](/emojis/cross-mark/) negative. More surprisingly for Americans, a [check mark](/emojis/check-mark/) is not universally positive: in Japan, and in some Nordic countries, a tick has traditionally been used to mark wrong answers. On a form for an international audience, write "yes" and "no."
- The [pile of poo](/emojis/pile-of-poo/) is cheerful in Japan partly because of a pun: a word for poop sounds like the word for luck, and golden poo charms are sold as lucky trinkets.
- The [eggplant](/emojis/eggplant/) is innuendo in English-language texting. In Japan it also has an innocent association: dreaming of an eggplant in the first dream of the new year is traditionally lucky, alongside Mount Fuji and a hawk.

Several objects make sense only with this background: the green-and-yellow shield 🔰 is the mark new drivers display on their cars in Japan, the 📛 name badge is the tulip-shaped tag worn by kindergarten children, and 🎏, 🎎 and 🎍 are decorations for specific Japanese holidays.

## Smiles, waves and tone in China

Chinese social platforms, WeChat above all, developed their own conventions, and several are close to the opposite of Western usage. These are widely reported by Chinese users and language teachers, and they are strongest among younger people.

- The [slightly smiling face](/emojis/slightly-smiling-face/) is often read as insincere, cold or contemptuous: a polite mask over displeasure. Younger users may take it as "I have nothing more to say to you." Older users frequently send it as a sincere smile, which produces family misunderstandings in China much like the ones English speakers have over the same emoji. See [passive-aggressive emojis](/guides/passive-aggressive-emojis/) for the Western version of this split.
- The [waving hand](/emojis/waving-hand/) can read as "goodbye, we are done here" instead of "hello."
- For warmth, a face with obvious joy, such as the [grinning face with smiling eyes](/emojis/grinning-face-with-smiling-eyes/), is the safer pick.

WeChat and other platforms also have built-in sticker sets that are not Unicode emojis at all, so a Chinese contact may be using symbols you cannot see or send from a standard keyboard.

## Colors, numbers and symbols

**Red and green in finance.** On Apple and several other platforms the [chart increasing](/emojis/chart-increasing/) emoji is drawn with a red line. That is not a mistake: in Japan, and also in mainland China, Taiwan and South Korea, red marks rising prices and green or blue marks falling ones, the reverse of the American and European convention. To a reader in Tokyo, a red upward chart is good news.

**Red more broadly.** Red is the color of luck and celebration in Chinese culture, which is why the red envelope 🧧 is a gift of money. Writing a living person's name in red, however, is avoided in Korea and parts of China because of associations with death.

**White.** White is a color of mourning in several East and South Asian traditions, and white flowers are funeral flowers in many countries. A [white heart](/emojis/white-heart/) is unlikely to offend anyone, but white floral imagery in a congratulations message can strike the wrong note. The [heart color guide](/guides/heart-emoji-colors-meaning/) covers the Western conventions.

**Numbers.** Four sounds like "death" in Chinese, Japanese and Korean and is avoided, while eight is lucky in Chinese culture. In Chinese texting, 520 sounds like "I love you" and 88 like "bye-bye." In Thai, the number five is pronounced "ha," so 555 is laughter.

**Gifts as symbols.** In Chinese culture, giving a clock is traditionally avoided because the phrase sounds like attending to a dying relative. An [alarm clock](/emojis/alarm-clock/) as a reminder is fine; a clock emoji as a birthday greeting is not ideal.

**The evil eye.** The [nazar amulet](/emojis/nazar-amulet/) is a protective charm in Turkey, Greece and across the Middle East and Mediterranean. Someone adding it to good news is guarding against envy, not sending something sinister.

**Nodding and shaking.** The [head shaking horizontally](/emojis/head-shaking-horizontally/) emoji means "no" to most of the world. In Bulgaria the conventions for nodding and shaking are famously reversed, and across South Asia a side-to-side head wobble signals agreement or acknowledgment.

## Animals, monkeys and insults

Animal emojis are common as affectionate nicknames, and that is where they misfire, because animal insults differ by language.

- Calling someone a dog or a pig is a serious insult in many cultures, and both animals are considered unclean in some religious traditions. A dog face that reads as "cute" between American friends may not travel.
- The three monkeys ([see-no-evil](/emojis/see-no-evil-monkey/), hear-no-evil, speak-no-evil) come from a Japanese proverb and a famous shrine carving. Western users mostly use them for embarrassment or "I didn't see that."
- Monkey and banana emojis directed at Black people are racist abuse. This is not a subtle cultural variation; it is a recognized form of harassment that platforms and sports bodies have had to act on repeatedly. Never use them as a comment on a person.
- The owl stands for wisdom in much of the West and is a bad omen in some other traditions, a reminder that even "neutral" animals carry baggage.

## Flags and politics

Flag emojis are their own minefield. Which territories have a flag emoji follows an international code list, not a political judgment by Unicode, and vendors sometimes hide particular flags in particular regions; Apple, for example, does not show the Taiwan flag on devices set to mainland China. Using a flag to label a language (a British or American flag for "English," a Spanish flag for Spanish spoken in Mexico) regularly irritates people who speak the language elsewhere. The mechanics are covered in [flag emojis explained](/guides/flag-emojis-explained/), and you can test your recognition with the [flag emoji quiz](/flag-emoji-quiz/).

## How to write for an international audience

A few habits remove most of the risk without making your messages sterile.

1. **Let words carry approval and refusal.** "Yes, approved" beats a gesture. Add the emoji afterward if you like.
2. **Prefer faces to hands.** A clearly happy face is understood nearly everywhere. Hands are where the regional insults are.
3. **Avoid irony.** Sarcastic smiles, the upside-down face and "skull for laughing" depend on shared slang. See [Gen Z emoji slang](/guides/gen-z-emoji-slang/) for how local that can be.
4. **Do not use an emoji to represent a nationality or religion you are not part of.** It is almost always received as stereotype.
5. **Mirror the other person.** If a contact in São Paulo sends you a thumbs up, you can send one back. Their usage tells you their reading.
6. **Ask when it matters.** Before a campaign or a mass announcement in another market, have a local reader check it. That step is routine in [marketing with emojis](/guides/emojis-in-marketing/).

### Quick reference: lower risk and higher risk

| Lower risk across cultures | Higher risk across cultures |
|---|---|
| Clearly joyful faces 😄 😊 | Hand gestures 👍 👌 🤘 🤞 |
| Celebration items 🎉 🎂 | The plain smile 🙂 and the wave 👋 as tone markers |
| Everyday objects ☕ 📅 📎 | Animals used to describe people |
| Hearts between friends and family | Flags used for languages |
| Weather and nature ☀️ 🌧️ | Slang uses (🍆 🍑 💀 🤡) |
| Words plus one emoji | Emoji-only replies to an important question |

When you are unsure, the rule of thumb is short: say it in words, decorate it with a face, and keep your hands out of it.
