The emojis most used for Christian faith are the Latin cross ✝️, folded hands 🙏, the dove 🕊️, the candle 🕯️, the church ⛪, and the open book 📖 standing in for the Bible. Only the crosses and the church are specifically Christian in design; the others are general symbols that carry Christian meaning because of scripture and long tradition. Which ones people reach for changes with the church year: candles in Advent, the cross in Holy Week, a sunrise at Easter, fire and the dove at Pentecost.

This guide explains each symbol, then goes through the seasons and feasts and the emojis associated with them. Practices differ between Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant churches, and the text notes where they do.

## The core symbols at a glance

| Emoji | Official name | Christian use |
|---|---|---|
| ✝️ | Latin cross | Christian faith; the crucifixion; Holy Week and Easter |
| ☦️ | Orthodox cross | Eastern Orthodox Christianity |
| ⛪ | Church | A church building, a service, a parish event |
| 🙏 | Folded hands | Prayer, thanks, a request for prayer |
| 🕊️ | Dove | The Holy Spirit; peace; baptism and confirmation |
| 🕯️ | Candle | Prayer, vigil, Advent, remembrance of the dead |
| 📖 | Open book | The Bible, a reading, a study group |
| 📿 | Prayer beads | The rosary (also beads of other religions) |
| 🔥 | Fire | Pentecost; the Holy Spirit |
| 👼 | Baby angel | Angels; cherubs in art |
| 😇 | Smiling face with halo | Innocence, usually joking; occasionally "saintly" |
| 🛐 | Place of worship | A generic sign for a prayer space, not tied to one religion |

## The cross emojis

There are two cross emojis with religious meaning. The [Latin cross](/emojis/latin-cross/) ✝️, with a longer lower arm, is the form used across Western Christianity, Catholic and Protestant alike. The Orthodox cross ☦️ has three horizontal bars: a short one at the top representing the inscription placed above Jesus, the main crossbar, and a slanted footrest near the bottom. It is the usual choice for Russian and other Eastern Orthodox contexts.

Some points that save confusion:

- **There is no crucifix emoji.** A crucifix shows the body of Christ on the cross; ✝️ is a plain cross. Catholics, who typically use the crucifix in church, use ✝️ in messages all the same.
- **There is no Celtic cross, no Jerusalem cross, and no fish (ichthys) symbol.** For the last, people use the ordinary fish 🐟.
- **✝️ is not ❌ or ➕.** The cross mark and the plus sign are separate characters with unrelated meanings.
- **The appearance varies.** Apple draws religious symbols as white glyphs on purple squares. Other vendors use their own styles. Because the cross is an older text symbol, in some apps it can also fall back to a plain black outline with no colored background.

In a profile bio, ✝️ is a simple statement that the person is Christian. In a message it accompanies a prayer, a verse, a baptism, or a feast day greeting. In obituaries and genealogies, the dagger sign † placed before a date marks a death; the emoji is sometimes used the same way, a convention found mostly in Christian contexts.

## Prayer: folded hands, candles, and beads

### Folded hands 🙏

The [folded hands](/emojis/folded-hands/) emoji is the workhorse of faith messaging. "Praying for you 🙏," "Prayers please 🙏," and "Thank you, Lord 🙏" are all standard. It came from Japanese emoji sets, where pressed palms express please and thank you, and it still carries those everyday meanings worldwide. So "Could you send the file 🙏" is not a prayer, and "🙏" as a reply to good news is usually thanks. The words around it settle the question. The emoji accepts skin tones, and its wider uses are covered in the guide to [hand gesture emoji meanings](/guides/hand-gesture-emojis-meaning/).

Related gestures appear in worship contexts too. Raising hands 🙌 is common for praise, "Hallelujah," and good news. Palms up together 🤲 shows open, cupped hands; it is strongly associated with Muslim prayer and is also used by Christians for receiving, offering, or petition. The person kneeling 🧎 can stand for kneeling in prayer.

### Candle 🕯️

The [candle](/emojis/candle/) covers a great deal of Christian practice: lighting a candle for someone in church, a vigil, the Advent wreath, the paschal candle lit at the Easter Vigil, and remembrance of the dead. "Lit a candle for your mom today 🕯️" is among the most common faith-related uses. Outside religion it means coziness or a power cut, so again the sentence decides.

### Prayer beads 📿

The Unicode name is deliberately general, since strings of beads are used for prayer in several religions. Catholics use it for the rosary: "Rosary at 7 tonight 📿." It pairs naturally with the [rose](/emojis/rose/) 🌹, a traditional symbol of Mary and the origin of the word rosary, and with a blue heart, blue being the color most associated with her in Western art. If you are inviting people to pray it, link a clear explainer such as Guide Catholic's [complete guide to the rosary](https://guidecatholic.com/blog/complete-rosary-guide/), since 📿 alone does not tell newcomers what to do.

## Symbols drawn from scripture and tradition

Many emojis used by Christians are ordinary pictures whose meaning comes from a specific text or custom.

- **🕊️ [Dove](/emojis/dove/).** The Gospels describe the Spirit descending like a dove at Jesus's baptism, and in Genesis a dove returns to Noah with an olive leaf. Most platforms draw the emoji carrying an olive branch, so it serves for both the Holy Spirit and peace.
- **🔥 [Fire](/emojis/fire/).** The Acts of the Apostles describes tongues of fire resting on the disciples at Pentecost. In everyday slang the same emoji means "excellent," so a caption like "Sunday's sermon 🔥" may be doing both jobs.
- **🐟 Fish.** The fish was an early Christian symbol; the Greek word for fish, ichthys, was read as an acrostic for "Jesus Christ, Son of God, Savior."
- **🐑 Lamb.** Jesus is called the Lamb of God, and the Good Shepherd is one of the oldest images in Christian art. The emoji is officially a ewe.
- **⚓ [Anchor](/emojis/anchor/).** An early symbol of hope, echoing the Letter to the Hebrews, which calls hope "an anchor of the soul."
- **🌈 [Rainbow](/emojis/rainbow/).** In Genesis, the sign of God's covenant after the flood. Today the emoji is used far more widely as a symbol of LGBTQ pride, so readers may take it either way.
- **🍞 🍷 Bread and wine.** Communion, the Eucharist, the Lord's Supper. The wine glass and grapes 🍇 both appear; there is no chalice emoji.
- **💧 Droplet.** Water, and therefore baptism.
- **👑 [Crown](/emojis/crown/).** Christ the King; also the crown of thorns in Holy Week messages and the Magi at Epiphany.
- **🔑 [Key](/emojis/key/).** Saint Peter, from the "keys of the kingdom" in Matthew's Gospel. Crossed keys are a symbol of the papacy.
- **🌅 [Sunrise](/emojis/sunrise/).** The resurrection, discovered at dawn; standard for Easter morning.
- **☘️ Shamrock.** Saint Patrick, who according to legend used its three leaves to explain the Trinity. The [four-leaf clover](/emojis/four-leaf-clover/) 🍀 is a different emoji and means luck.

### Angels and halos

The baby angel 👼 is a cherub of the kind seen in Renaissance painting. It is used for angels in general, for Christmas, and, with great tenderness, by families who have lost an infant or a pregnancy. Read it carefully in that setting.

The [smiling face with halo](/emojis/smiling-face-with-halo/) 😇 is almost always playful: "I would never 😇." It rarely refers to actual holiness, though people do use it to tease a friend for behaving well.

Western churches that follow a liturgical calendar assign a color to each season, shown in vestments and altar cloths. Many people echo those colors with hearts when posting about a season. ### Advent

In Western Christianity, Advent begins on the fourth Sunday before Christmas and is a season of preparation and expectation. Its best-known custom is the Advent wreath: four candles, one more lit each Sunday. In the common arrangement three are purple and one is rose, the rose candle belonging to the third Sunday, called Gaudete Sunday, when the tone turns to joy. Some wreaths add a white candle in the center for Christmas.

There is no wreath emoji, so people count the weeks in candles: 🕯️ for the first Sunday, 🕯️🕯️ for the second, and so on. A [purple heart](/emojis/purple-heart/) matches the season's color, and a [pink heart](/emojis/pink-heart/) is sometimes used for Gaudete. An example: "Third Sunday of Advent 🕯️🕯️🕯️ Rejoice!"

### Christmas and Epiphany

Christmas celebrates the birth of Jesus. Faith-centered greetings favor the [glowing star](/emojis/glowing-star/) 🌟 for the Star of Bethlehem, the baby angel, the church, and a [white heart](/emojis/white-heart/) for the liturgical color: "Christ is born! 🌟🤍." Epiphany, on January 6 in the traditional calendar, recalls the visit of the Magi, so crowns, camels, gifts, and stars appear. Some Orthodox churches that keep the Julian calendar celebrate Christmas on January 7. The secular set of trees, Santas, and snowmen is covered in [Christmas emojis and their meanings](/guides/christmas-emojis-meanings/).

### Lent and Holy Week

Lent is the penitential season before Easter, traditionally counted as forty days and marked by prayer, fasting, and almsgiving. In Western churches it begins on Ash Wednesday, when ashes are placed on the forehead, often in the shape of a cross. There is no ash emoji; ✝️ with 🙏 is the usual pairing. Eastern Orthodox churches begin Great Lent on a Monday, known as Clean Monday, and usually on different dates.

Lent is restrained, and its messages tend to be as well: ✝️ 💜 🙏, without party symbols. The fourth Sunday, Laetare Sunday, is the season's rose-colored pause, parallel to Gaudete in Advent.

Holy Week has a clear sequence:

1. **Palm Sunday.** Jesus's entry into Jerusalem. 🌿 or 🌴 for the palms.
2. **Holy Thursday (Maundy Thursday).** The Last Supper. 🍞🍷.
3. **Good Friday.** The crucifixion. ✝️, often alone, sometimes with a [black heart](/emojis/black-heart/) or a candle. It is a day of mourning, so "Happy Good Friday" with festive emojis strikes many Christians as off-key.
4. **Holy Saturday.** Waiting; the Easter Vigil that night begins with fire and the paschal candle. 🕯️🔥.
5. **Easter Sunday.** The resurrection.

### Easter and Pentecost

Easter is the principal feast of the Christian year. The religious set is ✝️ 🌅 🤍 🕯️ 🐑 with spring flowers; the cultural set is 🥚 🐣 🐰. Common greetings include "He is risen! ✝️🌅" and the traditional exchange, especially loved in Orthodox churches, "Christ is risen!" answered by "Truly He is risen!" Orthodox Easter, called Pascha, is calculated differently and often falls later than the Western date, so a greeting to an Orthodox friend may be due on another Sunday.

The Easter season lasts fifty days. The Ascension is marked on the fortieth day, a Thursday, though many dioceses move it to the following Sunday. The fiftieth day is Pentecost, celebrating the coming of the Holy Spirit on the disciples: 🔥🕊️ with a [red heart](/emojis/red-heart/) for the color of the day.

## Saints' days and Marian feasts

Feast days tend to borrow an emoji from the saint's story or patronage.

- **Saint Valentine, February 14.** Hearts, though the day is now largely secular.
- **Saint Patrick, March 17.** ☘️ 💚 and the Irish flag.
- **Saint Joseph, March 19.** 🔨 or 🪚 for the carpenter; 🌼 lilies by stand-in.
- **Saints Peter and Paul, June 29.** 🔑 for Peter, 📜 or 🗡️ for Paul.
- **The Assumption of Mary, August 15.** 🌹 💙 👑.
- **Saint Francis of Assisi, October 4.** Animals, 🐦 🐺, and 🕊️; many parishes bless pets around this date.
- **All Saints' Day, November 1, and All Souls' Day, November 2.** 🕯️ 🤍 for the saints, 🕯️ 🙏 for the faithful departed.
- **Saint Nicholas, December 6.** 🎁 👞 🍬 in countries where he brings gifts.
- **Our Lady of Guadalupe, December 12.** 🌹 and the Mexican flag.
- **Saint Lucy, December 13.** 🕯️ 👑.

Dates here follow the Roman Catholic calendar; Orthodox and some Protestant calendars place certain feasts on other days.

## Sacraments and life events

Milestones in church life have settled into recognizable combinations:

- **Baptism or christening:** 💧 🕊️ 🕯️ 🤍
- **First Communion:** 🍞 🍷 🙏 🤍
- **Confirmation:** 🔥 🕊️ ✝️
- **Wedding:** 💍 ⛪ 🤍 (💒 shows a wedding chapel with a heart)
- **Ordination or religious vows:** 🙏 ✝️ 📖
- **Funeral and condolence:** 🕯️ 🕊️ 🙏 ✝️

For condolences, a helpful rule is to match the family's own language. If the announcement says "funeral Mass" or quotes scripture, "Praying for you all ✝️🕯️" will be welcome. If you do not know their beliefs, 🕯️ 🕊️ 🤍 offers the same sympathy without presuming. Heart colors carry their own conventions outside church use, which the guide to [heart emoji colors](/guides/heart-emoji-colors-meaning/) lays out.

## Using faith emojis respectfully

Religious symbols are read as sincere statements, so a few habits are worth keeping, whether or not you share the faith.

- **Do not use the cross as decoration or as a joke.** To believers it refers to an execution and to the center of their faith.
- **Keep the mood of the day.** Good Friday and Ash Wednesday are solemn; Easter and Christmas are joyful. Emojis that fit one clash with the other.
- **Be accurate about traditions.** ☦️ for an Orthodox friend, the right date for their Easter, 📿 for a Catholic praying the rosary. Small accuracy reads as respect.
- **Remember that readers differ.** In mixed groups, such as a workplace chat, religious symbols can feel out of place to some colleagues; [emojis at work](/guides/emojis-at-work-etiquette/) discusses where the lines usually sit. Symbols also shift meaning between countries, as [emojis across cultures](/guides/emojis-across-cultures/) shows.
- **Offering prayer is not the same as imposing it.** "Praying for you 🙏" from a believer is a kindness in most settings. If you know the recipient would rather not, "Thinking of you 🤍" says what you mean.
