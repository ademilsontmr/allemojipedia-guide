New emojis are approved by the Unicode Consortium, a nonprofit that maintains the standard for digital text. Anyone can submit a proposal, a working group of volunteers and company representatives picks a small number each year, and after formal approval each platform draws its own version and ships it in a software update. The whole trip usually takes about two years.

That means the answer to "why isn't there an emoji for X?" is almost never "Apple hasn't made one." It is usually that nobody submitted a strong proposal, or that the idea ran into one of the rules below.

## Who actually decides

Three groups are involved, and they do different jobs.

**The Unicode Consortium** is a nonprofit based in California. Its main work is not emoji at all: it maintains the Unicode Standard, which assigns a code point to every character in the world's writing systems so that text looks the same on every device. Emoji are a small, very visible part of that work.

**The emoji working group** handles the proposals. For most of its history it was called the Emoji Subcommittee; it now goes by the Emoji Standard & Research Working Group. It reads submissions, asks proposers for changes, and assembles a recommended list for each year.

**The Unicode Technical Committee (UTC)** gives final approval. Voting rights belong to the consortium's members, which include Apple, Google, Microsoft and Meta as well as other companies and institutions. This is the practical check on the system: there is little point approving a character that the major platforms will not agree to ship.

After approval, the companies switch roles from voters to **vendors**. Unicode provides a name, a code point and a sample image. It does not provide the artwork on your phone. Each vendor's design team draws its own, which is why [emojis look different on iPhone and Android](/guides/iphone-vs-android-emojis/).

## What a proposal has to contain

Proposals are documents, not suggestions typed into a form field. Unicode publishes submission guidelines, and proposals that do not follow them are returned. The core requirements:

- **A name and keywords** for the proposed emoji.
- **A sample image** in color and in black and white, which you created or have the rights to license. This is only a reference; vendors will redraw it.
- **A suggested category and position**, such as where it would sit among the [food and drink emojis](/categories/food-and-drink/).
- **Evidence of expected usage**, the section that decides most proposals.
- **Answers to the selection factors**, explaining why the idea meets the criteria for inclusion and does not trip the criteria for exclusion.

Submissions are accepted only during an annual window. In recent years it has opened in early April and closed at the end of July. Check the dates on Unicode's site before you write anything, because the guidelines and the list of categories being prioritized change from year to year.

### How "expected usage" is measured

Unicode asks proposers to show that people already talk about the concept a lot. The standard method is search data: you compare how often your term is searched for and written about against a reference emoji that Unicode treats as a benchmark for ordinary popularity. Screenshots of trend comparisons go into the proposal.

Two things do not count. Petitions are explicitly excluded, however many signatures they have. So are tallies of people asking for the emoji on social media. The logic is that campaigns measure enthusiasm at one moment, while an emoji is permanent: once a character is encoded, Unicode's stability policies mean it is never removed.

## What makes an emoji likely to be approved

Unicode's selection factors for inclusion can be summarized as five questions.

1. **Will it be used a lot?** Backed by the search evidence above.
2. **Does it have more than one meaning?** Emoji that work as metaphors do better than literal objects. A [melting face](/emojis/melting-face/) can mean heat, embarrassment, dread or sarcasm. A shark can mean the animal or a ruthless person.
3. **Is it visually distinct?** The image has to be recognizable at the size of a line of text and not be confused with an existing emoji.
4. **Does it fill a gap?** A new emoji should say something that cannot already be said. The plain [pink heart](/emojis/pink-heart/) was approved in 2022 partly because pink was an obvious hole in the heart colors.
5. **Does it complete a set, or is it needed for compatibility?** Emoji are sometimes added because a widely used platform already has them.

In recent years the working group has also said it favors emoji that work in combination with others and has been approving fewer new characters per year than it did in the late 2010s. Some recent annual releases have contained fewer than ten entirely new concepts.

## What gets rejected, and why

The exclusion factors are just as specific. A proposal is in trouble if the idea is:

- **Overly specific.** One emoji for sushi is fine; one for each type of sushi is not. This is also why there is one [dog face](/emojis/dog-face/) rather than an emoji per breed, with a few historical exceptions.
- **Open-ended.** If approving it implies approving dozens of similar ones (every sport, every profession, every dish), it needs a reason why this one stands out.
- **Already representable.** If an existing emoji or a short combination does the job, a new one is unnecessary. A proposal for "tired worker" competes with the [weary face](/emojis/weary-face/) plus a laptop.
- **A logo, brand, or specific product.** No company marks, no UI icons, no signage.
- **A specific person or deity.** No historical figures, celebrities, or gods. General religious symbols such as the [latin cross](/emojis/latin-cross/) exist, but figures do not.
- **Transient.** Memes and fads rarely qualify, because the emoji will outlive them.
- **A flag.** In 2022 Unicode announced that it would no longer accept proposals for new flag emoji of any kind. Country flags are still added automatically when a region receives an official code. The reasoning is in [flag emojis explained](/guides/flag-emojis-explained/).

Being a good cause is not a selection factor either way. Proposals connected to representation have succeeded when they also showed broad use, and failed when they did not.

Candidates can also be dropped late. In 2017 a "frowning pile of poo" made the shortlist as a companion to the smiling [pile of poo](/emojis/pile-of-poo/) and was withdrawn after pointed objections from members of the technical committee, who argued it opened the door to endless emotional variants of objects. In 2016 a rifle that had been scheduled to become an emoji was removed from the emoji list shortly before release, though it remained in Unicode as an ordinary symbol.

## The timeline from proposal to phone

The dates shift a little each year, but the shape is consistent.

| Stage | Roughly when | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Submission window | April to July, year 1 | Proposals are submitted using Unicode's form |
| Review | Rest of year 1 | The working group evaluates, requests revisions, declines most |
| Candidate list | During year 2 | A shortlist is published as draft candidates; details can still change |
| Final approval | September, year 3 | Code points and names are fixed in a Unicode and Emoji release |
| Vendor design | Before and after approval | Each platform draws its artwork, often starting from the draft list |
| On your phone | Late year 3 to mid year 4 | Shipped in operating system and app updates |

For a concrete case, the emoji approved in September 2025, including distorted face and fight cloud, came out of proposals submitted about two years earlier and reached most phones between late 2025 and 2026. That release is covered in [new emojis in 2026](/guides/new-emojis-2026/).

### Why the last step is slow

Approval is a line in a data file. Getting the emoji to you requires:

1. **Artwork** in the vendor's house style, in every skin tone and gender variant where applicable.
2. **Font and keyboard updates**, including search keywords in dozens of languages. Those names and keywords come from CLDR, Unicode's locale data project.
3. **A software release.** Emoji fonts ship with the operating system on most devices, so they wait for a scheduled update. Apple has typically added each year's emoji in a spring point release. Android phones depend on the manufacturer, so a Pixel and a Samsung phone can get the same emoji months apart.

Until both sender and receiver have updated, a new emoji may arrive as an empty box. That gap is explained in [why emojis show as boxes](/guides/why-emojis-show-as-boxes/).

## New characters versus sequences

Not every new emoji is a new character. Many are **sequences**: existing characters glued together so that supporting devices show a single image.

- **Skin tones** are a base emoji followed by one of five modifier characters. See [emoji skin tones explained](/guides/emoji-skin-tones-explained/).
- **ZWJ sequences** join emoji with an invisible zero width joiner. The [rainbow flag](/emojis/rainbow-flag/) is a white flag joined to a rainbow, and [heart on fire](/emojis/heart-on-fire/) is a heart joined to fire. Professions, families and most gendered emoji work this way.
- **Flag sequences** pair regional indicator letters.

Sequences matter to the approval process for two reasons. First, they do not consume code points and they fall back gracefully: on an old device, a new sequence shows as its component emoji side by side instead of a blank box. Second, Unicode can publish sequence-only updates between major versions, which is how [face exhaling](/emojis/face-exhaling/) and [mending heart](/emojis/mending-heart/) arrived in a 2020 release that added no new characters at all.

The set of sequences Unicode asks all vendors to support is called **RGI**, for "recommended for general interchange." A vendor can technically support extra sequences of its own, but they will only display properly on that vendor's platform, so few do.

## Proposals that changed the keyboard

Many familiar emoji began as documents written by people outside the big technology companies.

- **Dumpling.** Journalist Jennifer 8. Lee and designer Yiying Lu proposed it after noticing its absence, and it was approved in 2017. Lee went on to co-found Emojination, a group that helps the public write proposals.
- **Person with headscarf.** Proposed in 2016 by Rayouf Alhumedhi, then a teenager living in Germany, and approved the following year.
- **Drop of blood.** Backed by the charity Plan International UK as a way to talk about menstruation, approved in 2019.
- **Accessibility emoji.** Apple submitted a proposal in 2018 for wheelchairs, a guide dog, a hearing aid and prosthetic limbs, developed with disability organizations. They were approved in 2019.
- **Mixed skin tone couples.** A campaign supported by the dating app Tinder led to couples holding hands with different skin tones in 2019.

The common thread is not the size of the campaign. Each proposal identified a clear gap, showed evidence of use, and proposed a design that worked at small size.

## What Unicode does not control

A few things people often assume are decided by Unicode are not.

**The artwork.** When Apple turned its pistol into a [water pistol](/emojis/water-pistol/) in 2016, that was Apple's decision alone. Other vendors followed later of their own accord.

**What an emoji means.** Unicode names characters; usage decides meaning. Nobody approved the [skull](/emojis/skull/) as a laughing emoji or the [eggplant](/emojis/eggplant/) as innuendo. For how meanings actually develop, see [Gen Z emoji slang](/guides/gen-z-emoji-slang/).

**Which emoji a platform hides or blocks.** Platforms decide what appears in their keyboards and search suggestions in each region.

**Stickers, mashups and generated images.** Google's [Emoji Kitchen](/guides/emoji-kitchen-guide/) combinations, messaging app stickers and AI-generated custom emoji are pictures sent as images. They need no approval, and they do not work as text in a document, a username or a search box.

## A checklist before you write a proposal

If you are serious about submitting, work through this list first. Most rejected ideas fail one of the first three points.

1. **Search the existing set.** Check the [full emoji list on unicode.org](https://unicode.org/emoji/charts/full-emoji-list.html) and try the obvious combinations. If two existing emoji express it, stop.
2. **Check the exclusions.** No brands, people, deities, flags, or fads.
3. **Test for multiple meanings.** Write down at least two or three distinct things people would use it to say. If you can only think of the literal one, the proposal is weak.
4. **Gather usage evidence** in the format the current guidelines ask for, compared against the reference emoji they specify.
5. **Draw it small.** Shrink your sample image to text size. If it is not recognizable, simplify it.
6. **Read the current guidelines on the [Unicode emoji pages](https://home.unicode.org/emoji/)** and note the submission window dates and any categories that are closed for the year.
7. **Plan for two years.** Even a successful proposal will not appear on phones soon, and you may be asked for revisions along the way.

One last rule of thumb: the committee is choosing vocabulary, not illustrations. The proposals that succeed describe something people need to say, and then show the picture that says it.
