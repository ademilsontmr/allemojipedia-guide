Emojis are acceptable in most workplaces as long as they do a job: acknowledging a message, showing warmth in a short reply, or marking status. The etiquette comes down to three habits. Use reactions for acknowledgment, mirror the person you are talking to, and keep emojis away from bad news, formal decisions, and anyone you have not yet built a rapport with.

The rest of this guide covers how that works in Slack, Microsoft Teams, and email, and how it changes with your boss, with clients, and across cultures and generations.

## Why emojis belong in work chat at all

Written workplace messages lose the tone that a voice or a face would carry. "Can you fix this today" reads as an order or as a casual request depending on who imagines it. A single [slightly smiling face](/emojis/slightly-smiling-face/) or [folded hands](/emojis/folded-hands/) settles which one you meant, in less space than a sentence of politeness.

Emojis at work do four useful things:

- **Acknowledge.** A reaction says "seen" without a reply that pings everyone in the channel.
- **Set tone.** They mark a blunt sentence as friendly, or a joke as a joke.
- **Signal status.** A [check mark button](/emojis/check-mark-button/) on a request means it is done; the [eyes](/emojis/eyes/) mean someone is on it.
- **Celebrate.** A row of [party poppers](/emojis/party-popper/) under a launch announcement is quick, visible recognition.

They cause trouble when they replace information ("🔥🔥🔥" as feedback on a proposal tells the author nothing), when they carry a meaning the reader does not share, or when they make something serious look flippant.

## The safe set and the risky set

Most workplace emoji use draws on a small, predictable group. If you are new to a team, staying inside it for the first few weeks costs you nothing.

| Emoji | Usual workplace meaning | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| 👍 | Got it, approved, sounds good | Can look curt as the only reply to a long message |
| ✅ | Done, confirmed | Low |
| 👀 | Looking at it now | Meaning is a team convention; confirm it |
| 🙏 | Thank you, or please | Low; read as prayer by some people |
| 🙌 | Great work, celebration | Low |
| 🎉 | Congratulations | Low; wrong on sensitive announcements |
| 🙂 | Friendly, polite | Some readers see a forced smile |
| 😂 | That was funny | Fine on jokes, harmful on someone's mistake |
| 💀 | "I'm dead" (laughing) | Colleagues unfamiliar with the slang read it literally |
| 😉 | Kidding, or flirtation | Avoid outside close peers |

A few notes on that table. The [thumbs up](/emojis/thumbs-up/) is the workhorse of workplace chat, but it has a generational wrinkle that the [passive-aggressive emojis guide](/guides/passive-aggressive-emojis/) covers in detail. The [skull](/emojis/skull/) and [loudly crying face](/emojis/loudly-crying-face/) as laughter are standard among younger staff and confusing to many others; the [Gen Z emoji slang guide](/guides/gen-z-emoji-slang/) explains where that came from. And the [winking face](/emojis/winking-face/) is the emoji most likely to turn an innocent sentence into one that sounds suggestive, which is reason enough to retire it at work.

Emojis that have no workplace use:

- Anything romantic or flirtatious: kiss faces, heart eyes, the [smirking face](/emojis/smirking-face/).
- Food and objects with sexual double meanings, such as the [eggplant](/emojis/eggplant/) and [peach](/emojis/peach/). If you are discussing an actual lunch order, the word is safer than the picture.
- Mocking faces aimed at a person: the clown, the eye roll, the nauseated face.
- The [middle finger](/emojis/middle-finger/), even as a joke between friends in a channel that others can read.

Hearts sit in between. A red heart reaction on a colleague's good news is common on many teams and unremarkable. A red heart sent privately to one colleague, repeatedly, is a different message. If you want warmth without ambiguity, the raised hands or a simple thank-you does the same work.

## Slack: reactions, custom emoji, and conventions

Slack is where workplace emoji culture is most developed, mostly because of reactions (Slack calls them "reacji"). You add one by hovering over a message and choosing the reaction button on desktop, or by pressing and holding the message on mobile. A reaction shows as a small counter under the message and does not send a channel notification the way a reply does.

That makes reactions the right tool for:

1. **Acknowledging an announcement.** Twenty people replying "Thanks!" is twenty notifications. Twenty reactions is none.
2. **Voting.** "React 1️⃣ for Tuesday, 2️⃣ for Thursday" is the fastest poll a team can run.
3. **Claiming and closing work.** Eyes for "I have this," check mark for "finished."
4. **Agreeing.** Adding to an existing reaction is a quiet "+1."

Slack also lets workspaces add custom emoji, unless an admin has restricted it. Custom emoji are where team in-jokes live, and they are a real part of belonging on a team. Two cautions: a custom emoji built from a coworker's face or an embarrassing moment needs that person's genuine consent, and newcomers have no way to know what a private joke means. If a custom emoji carries operational meaning (for example one that marks a message as needing a manager's sign-off), document it.

Some teams go further and attach automations to reactions, so that a specific emoji files a ticket or copies a message to another channel. Whether that is available depends on your plan and on what your admins have set up. Where it exists, a reaction is an action with consequences, so check before you use an unfamiliar emoji in an operational channel.

You can type any emoji in Slack by name between colons, such as `:thumbsup:`, and you can set a default skin tone in your preferences. For the mechanics on each operating system, see [how to type emojis on any device](/guides/how-to-type-emojis-on-any-device/).

## Microsoft Teams: similar tools, more formal rooms

Teams supports reactions in much the same way. Hover over a message on desktop or press and hold on mobile, and you can pick from a short row of quick reactions or open the full emoji picker. Older versions of Teams offered only six fixed reactions (like, heart, laugh, surprised, sad, angry), so colleagues who have used Teams for years may still treat reactions as a narrow set. Newer versions allow the wider emoji library, and features such as custom emoji depend on your organization's settings.

Teams is often deployed in larger, more traditional organizations and is tied closely to meetings, so the emoji norms tend to run a notch more conservative than on Slack:

- In **meeting chat**, reactions and a raised hand are useful and low-risk. A stream of emoji commentary during a senior person's presentation is not.
- In **channels with wide membership**, assume people you have never met, including executives, will read the thread.
- In **one-to-one chat**, follow the other person's lead exactly as you would in a text conversation.

Live meeting reactions (the hearts, applause, and laughs that float over the screen) are public and immediate. Applause at the end of a presentation is welcome. A laugh reaction while someone is explaining a problem can look like mockery even if you were reacting to something else.

## Email: where one emoji is usually the limit

Email is more formal than chat for a practical reason: it gets forwarded, quoted, and archived, and it reaches people who have none of the original context. An emoji that was friendly between two colleagues can look careless three forwards later in front of a director.

A workable rule set:

- **Internal, casual email** to people you know: one emoji is fine. "Thanks for turning this around so fast 🙌" reads as warm.
- **First contact, applications, proposals, contracts, complaints, HR matters:** none.
- **Subject lines:** leave emojis out of person-to-person work email. Marketing email is a separate discipline with its own trade-offs, covered in [emojis in marketing](/guides/emojis-in-marketing/).
- **Signatures:** skip them. A decorative emoji in a signature repeats on every message, including the serious ones.

Both Gmail and Outlook now offer emoji reactions to email in at least some versions and account types. They are handy inside one organization, but treat them cautiously with outsiders. A recipient on a different mail system may not see the reaction at all, or may receive it as a separate, slightly odd email saying that you reacted. With external contacts, a short written reply is more reliable.

There is also a rendering issue. Emojis are drawn differently by Apple, Google, Microsoft, and Samsung, and some desktop mail software shows older or flatter designs. A face that looks gently amused on your phone can look smug on the recipient's laptop, and a recent emoji may arrive as an empty box. [iPhone vs. Android emojis](/guides/iphone-vs-android-emojis/) shows how far the designs can drift, and [why emojis show as boxes](/guides/why-emojis-show-as-boxes/) explains the missing-character problem.

## With your boss: mirror, then stay one step behind

Power changes how emojis read. A manager who sends a thumbs up is being efficient. An employee who answers a detailed request from a manager with only a thumbs up can look dismissive, even though the symbol is identical.

Practical guidance for messaging upward:

- **Let them go first.** If your manager uses emojis, matching their style is safe. If they never do, keep yours rare.
- **Pair the emoji with words** when the message matters. "Will do, you'll have it by 3 👍" beats the emoji alone.
- **Drop emojis for hard conversations.** Asking for a raise, pushing back on a decision, reporting a mistake, or giving notice all call for plain sentences.
- **Do not use a smile to cushion a complaint.** "I guess I'll just work the weekend again 🙂" is the pattern people describe as passive-aggressive.

If you manage people, the same logic runs the other way. Your emoji carry extra weight. A bare [neutral face](/emojis/neutral-face/) or a thumbs up on work someone spent a week on can be read as disapproval. A single [thinking face](/emojis/thinking-face/) on a proposal, with no comment, will worry its author for the rest of the day. Managers also set the norm: a team whose lead reacts warmly and specifically tends to do the same. And never deliver criticism, a rejection, or bad news with an emoji attached; "Unfortunately we're not moving forward with your promotion this cycle 😕" is worse than the sentence alone.

## With clients and people outside the company

With clients, vendors, recruiters, and anyone else outside your organization, begin with no emojis and let the relationship decide. Many client relationships become warm and emoji-friendly within weeks, particularly in shared Slack channels or on WhatsApp. The point is that the client sets that pace.

- **Mirror their register.** If a client signs off with a smile, a smile back is fine. Do not escalate from their one smile to your three hearts.
- **Keep emojis off anything about money, scope, deadlines, or fault.** An invoice reminder with a winking face is not friendlier; it is confusing.
- **Be careful with agreement emojis.** In 2023 a Canadian court (South West Terminal Ltd. v. Achter Land & Cattle Ltd., in Saskatchewan) held that a thumbs up sent in reply to a photographed contract counted as acceptance of it, and the farmer who sent it was ordered to pay damages. Courts elsewhere may see it differently, but the practical lesson travels: if you only mean "received," write "received, will review."
- **Assume screenshots.** Client messages are shared internally on their side as well as yours.

## Culture, generation, and accessibility

Three kinds of difference cause most workplace emoji misreadings.

**Culture.** Gestures do not travel evenly. The thumbs up has traditionally been considered rude in parts of the Middle East and West Africa, the [OK hand](/emojis/ok-hand/) is offensive in several countries, and folded hands can be read as thanks, a request, or prayer. On an international team, prefer faces and symbols such as the check mark over hand gestures. [Emojis across cultures](/guides/emojis-across-cultures/) has the country-by-country detail.

**Generation.** Broadly, older colleagues tend to use emojis literally, so a smile is a smile, while younger colleagues are more likely to treat some of the same emojis as ironic or cold and to use the skull for laughter. Neither reading is wrong. When a message from someone of a different generation lands oddly, assume the literal, friendly meaning first.

**Accessibility.** Screen readers announce each emoji by its name, so "Great job 👏👏👏👏👏" is read aloud as "clapping hands" five times. Use one, place it at the end of the sentence, and do not substitute emojis for words that carry meaning. Skin-tone choices come up here too: most people pick the tone closest to their own or leave the default yellow, as discussed in [emoji skin tones explained](/guides/emoji-skin-tones-explained/). More on writing for assistive technology is in the [emoji accessibility guide](/guides/emoji-accessibility-screen-readers/).

## When to leave emojis out entirely

Some messages should contain none, whatever the team culture:

- Layoffs, terminations, disciplinary matters, and performance warnings.
- Condolences, illness, and personal hardship. A [red heart](/emojis/red-heart/) may be right between close colleagues; with anyone else, words are safer.
- Security incidents, outages affecting customers, and legal matters.
- Any message you would be uncomfortable seeing read aloud in a meeting or quoted in a dispute.

Workplace chat is a business record. Messages in Slack and Teams can be exported by administrators under certain plans and policies, and they can surface in HR investigations or litigation. An emoji in that setting is evidence of tone and intent like any other part of the message. Repeatedly sending suggestive emojis to a colleague can form part of a harassment complaint in the same way suggestive words can.

## A one-minute check before you send

Run through this list when you are unsure:

1. **Would the message still be clear with the emoji removed?** If not, add words.
2. **Has this person used emojis with me first?** If not, and they are senior or external, hold back.
3. **Is the news good or neutral?** If it is bad, sensitive, or about money, remove the emoji.
4. **Could it be read as flirtation, sarcasm, or mockery by someone who dislikes me?** If so, change it.
5. **Is a reaction enough?** If you are only acknowledging, react instead of replying.
6. **One is plenty.** A second and third emoji rarely add meaning, and they slow down anyone using a screen reader.

When none of those questions raises a flag, send it. An accurate, friendly emoji in the right place makes workplace writing clearer, and that is the whole case for using them.
