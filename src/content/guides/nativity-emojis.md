There is no single nativity emoji, so the manger scene is built from pieces: 👶 for the Christ Child, 🛖 for the stable, 🐂 and 🫏 for the ox and donkey, 🐑 for the shepherds' flock, 👼 for the angels, 👑 and 🐪 for the Magi, and 🌟 for the Star of Bethlehem, which also has no emoji of its own. A short line such as 🌟🛖👶🐂🫏🐑 is instantly readable as a crèche to anyone who has seen one.

This guide goes figure by figure through the scene, explains which emoji works best for each and why, and ends with combinations and captions for parish pages, Advent posts, and family chats. The broader set of Christmas symbols, from trees to Santa, is covered in [Christmas emojis and their meanings](/guides/christmas-emojis-meanings/), and faith symbols across the whole church year are in [Christian emojis and their meanings](/guides/christian-emojis-meanings/).

## The nativity scene, figure by figure

The custom of setting up a nativity scene is usually traced to Saint Francis of Assisi, who staged a living crèche with a real ox and donkey at Greccio, Italy, in 1223. The tradition spread from there and today goes by many names: crèche, crib, presepio, belén, pesebre. The emoji version follows the same cast.

| Figure | Best emoji | Alternatives | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Christ Child | 👶 | 👼 | The center of every scene; put it in the middle of the line |
| Mary | 👩‍🍼 | 🤱 👩 | No named Mary emoji; 💙 is often added for Marian blue |
| Joseph | 🧔 | 👨 👴 | A bearded man matches most artistic tradition |
| The Holy Family | 👪 | 👨‍👩‍👦 | Family emoji; designs vary by platform |
| Stable or cave | 🛖 | 🏚️ 🌾 | 🛖 is officially "hut"; 🌾 suggests straw |

A few of these deserve a closer look.

**The Christ Child.** The [baby](/emojis/baby/) emoji is the obvious and best choice. Some people use the [baby angel](/emojis/baby-angel/) 👼 instead because it already looks luminous, but that blurs the Child with the angels; keep 👼 for the heavenly host and 👶 for Jesus.

**The stable.** The Gospel of Luke says Mary laid the newborn in a manger "because there was no room for them in the inn," and later tradition places the birth in a stable or, in the Eastern tradition, a cave. The [hut](/emojis/hut/) 🛖, added to Unicode in 2020, is drawn as a small round dwelling with a thatched or wooden roof and makes a convincing stable. There is no manger emoji; 🌾 (sheaf of rice) next to 👶 reads as straw.

**The ox and donkey.** Neither animal is mentioned in the Gospel accounts of the birth. They come from tradition, which connected the scene with Isaiah 1:3, where the ox knows its owner and the donkey knows its master's manger. The [ox](/emojis/ox/) 🐂 has been in the emoji set for years; the [donkey](/emojis/donkey/) 🫏 only arrived in Emoji 15.0 (2022). On phones or apps that have not been updated since, it can appear as an empty box, which is explained in [why emojis show as boxes](/guides/why-emojis-show-as-boxes/). If your audience includes grandparents with older phones, 🐴 is a safe fallback.

**The sheep.** Unicode has two: the [ewe](/emojis/ewe/) 🐑, officially just "ewe" and drawn as a fluffy white sheep, and the [ram](/emojis/ram/) 🐏, with curled horns. 🐑 is the standard flock emoji. It also carries the image of Jesus as the Lamb of God, which is why it appears in Easter posts as well.

All of these animals live in the [animals and nature](/categories/animals-and-nature/) category of the emoji keyboard, and the people figures are under [people and body](/categories/people-and-body/).

## The Holy Family in people emojis

There are no emojis named for Mary, Joseph, or Jesus, so the Holy Family is drawn from the general people set. That turns out to be a strength: every people emoji below supports the five skin tone modifiers, so you can make the figures look like the ones in your church or like the many cultural traditions of nativity art, from Filipino belén sets to Ethiopian icons. If you have never changed a tone, [emoji skin tones explained](/guides/emoji-skin-tones-explained/) shows where the setting is on each keyboard.

Common ways to write the family:

- **👩‍🍼👶🧔** Mary holding the Child, with Joseph beside her. The woman-feeding-baby emoji shows a mother with a bottle, which some people find too modern; 🤱 (breast-feeding) is a more traditional image of the Madonna and Child, and many classical paintings show exactly that.
- **🧔👶👩** A simple three-figure line with the Child in the center, the way a crèche is usually arranged.
- **👪** The single family emoji. It is quick, but platforms draw it differently and it can look like a modern household rather than the Holy Family. Pair it with 🌟 or 🛖 for context.

Joined emojis such as 👩‍🍼 and 👨‍👩‍👦 are built from several characters glued together. On older software they can split apart into separate figures, so a simple sequence of individual people is the most reliable way to post to a large parish audience.

Catholics often add a [blue heart](/emojis/blue-heart/) 💙 for Mary, reflecting the blue mantle she wears in much religious art, and a [white heart](/emojis/white-heart/) 🤍 for the white of the Christmas season. The Feast of the Holy Family falls on the Sunday after Christmas (or December 30 when there is no Sunday in between), which makes it a natural day for a 👩👶🧔🤍 post.

## The Star of Bethlehem: no emoji, three good stand-ins

The Gospel of Matthew tells of a star that went before the Magi until "it stopped over the place where the child was." Unicode has never encoded a specific Star of Bethlehem, so people pick from the star emojis that exist.

- **🌟 Glowing star.** The most common choice. The [glowing star](/emojis/glowing-star/) is drawn with rays or a soft halo on most platforms, which suits a guiding light.
- **⭐ Star.** The plain [star](/emojis/star/) is cleaner and works well in rows, for example at the top of a stacked emoji "crèche." It also doubles as the tree-topper star, so it reads as Christmas even outside a religious context.
- **🌠 Shooting star.** Many paintings and Christmas cards show the Star of Bethlehem with a tail, like a comet, and 🌠 captures that look. Note that 🌠 often carries a "make a wish" meaning, so it works best next to other nativity symbols.

Add [sparkles](/emojis/sparkles/) ✨ or the dizzy symbol 💫 to make any of these shine, and place the star above or before the rest of the scene. The [night with stars](/emojis/night-with-stars/) 🌃 sets the scene at night, which fits "Silent Night" and Midnight Mass posts, though be aware that it shows a city skyline, not a village.

One thing to avoid: ✡️, the Star of David, is a Jewish symbol and is not a stand-in for the Christmas star.

## Shepherds and angels: the Gospel of Luke

Luke's Gospel gives the scene most people picture: shepherds "keeping watch over their flock by night," an angel announcing "good news of great joy," and then "a multitude of the heavenly host" praising God. In emoji that sequence is easy to tell.

- **Shepherds.** There is no shepherd emoji. The farmer 🧑‍🌾 is the closest figure, and most people simply let the flock speak for them: 🐑🐑🐏 with a campfire 🔥 and 🌃 for the night watch.
- **The angel's announcement.** The [baby angel](/emojis/baby-angel/) 👼 is the standard angel symbol, despite being drawn as a cherub. A trumpet 🎺 or musical notes 🎶 suggest the heavenly song, often summed up in the Latin "Gloria in excelsis Deo."
- **😇 Smiling face with halo.** In everyday chat the [smiling face with halo](/emojis/smiling-face-with-halo/) usually means "who, me? I'm innocent," often jokingly. Next to 👼 and 🌟 it reads as angelic, but on its own it can look playful rather than devout.

A shepherds' line might read: 🌃🐑🐑🔥 ➡️ 👼✨🎶 ➡️ 🛖👶🌟. Arrows make the story order clear, a technique described in more detail in [emoji combos and ideas](/guides/emoji-combos-ideas/).

## The Magi and Epiphany (January 6)

The visit of the Magi comes from the Gospel of Matthew, not Luke. Matthew calls them Magi "from the East," says they followed a star, and lists three gifts: gold, frankincense, and myrrh. He does not say how many there were or that they were kings. The tradition of three kings, often named Caspar, Melchior, and Balthasar, grew up later from the three gifts and from Old Testament passages such as Psalm 72 and Isaiah 60, which also mentions camels.

The Church celebrates their visit at Epiphany, traditionally January 6, the twelfth day after Christmas. In the United States and some other countries, Catholic dioceses keep the Solemnity of the Epiphany on the Sunday between January 2 and January 8. In Spain and much of Latin America, January 6 is Three Kings' Day (Día de Reyes), when children receive gifts. Many Orthodox Christians on the Julian calendar celebrate Christmas itself on January 7.

Emoji for the Magi:

- **👑 Crown.** The [crown](/emojis/crown/) is the default sign of the kings, usually tripled: 👑👑👑.
- **🐪 and 🐫 Camels.** The [camel](/emojis/camel/) 🐪 has one hump (a dromedary) and 🐫 has two. Either works.
- **🫅 Person with crown and 🤴 prince.** These show the kings as people and take skin tones, which lets you follow the common artistic tradition of depicting the three Magi with different appearances.
- **The gifts.** There is no emoji for frankincense or myrrh. The [wrapped gift](/emojis/wrapped-gift/) 🎁 covers all three, and 🪙 (coin) can stand for gold.
- **The journey.** 🏜️ desert, 🧭 compass, and the [palm tree](/emojis/palm-tree/) 🌴 sketch the road from the East.

**Chalking the door.** A widespread Epiphany custom is blessing the home and writing the year and the initials C, M, B over the door in chalk, for example 20 + C + M + B + 27. The letters are read both as the traditional names of the Magi and as the Latin "Christus mansionem benedicat," "May Christ bless this house." There is no chalk emoji; people use 🏠 or 🚪 with the inscription typed out, sometimes adding 🖍️ (crayon) as the nearest writing tool.

## Advent: building the scene week by week

Advent, the four weeks of preparation before Christmas, begins on the fourth Sunday before December 25. In 2026 that is November 29, with the third Sunday, Gaudete Sunday, on December 13. The season's color is violet, and many households keep an Advent wreath with three violet candles and one rose candle.

Many families also set up the crèche gradually: the stable and animals first, Mary and Joseph traveling across the room toward it, the Child placed on Christmas Eve or Christmas morning, and the Magi moved a little closer each day until Epiphany. Emoji posts can follow the same rhythm, which keeps a parish page or family chat from posting the full scene too early.

| When | Emoji line | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| First Sunday of Advent | 🕯️💜 🛖🐂🫏 | The stable is ready and waiting |
| Second Sunday | 🕯️🕯️💜 🫏👩🧔 | Mary and Joseph on the road to Bethlehem |
| Gaudete Sunday | 🕯️🕯️🩷 🐑🐑🌃 | The shepherds in the fields; rejoice |
| Fourth Sunday | 🕯️🕯️🕯️🕯️ 👼✨ | The angel's message is near |
| Christmas Eve and Day | 🌟🛖👶🐂🫏🐑👼 | The Child is born; the scene is complete |
| Epiphany | 👑👑👑🐪🌟🎁 | The Magi arrive |

The [candle](/emojis/candle/) counts the weeks, the [purple heart](/emojis/purple-heart/) matches the liturgical color, and the [pink heart](/emojis/pink-heart/) 🩷 stands in for the rose candle of Gaudete. More on Advent colors and customs is in the Christian emojis guide linked above.

## Tips for respectful, readable nativity posts

**Keep the Child at the center.** In a crèche, everything turns toward the manger. A line where 👶 sits between Mary and Joseph, with the animals and star around it, mirrors that arrangement and reads naturally.

**Be careful with joke emojis.** 😇 can read as playful, and 🧙 (mage) is drawn as a fantasy wizard, not a Magus. Santa 🎅 in the middle of a nativity line mixes two very different images of Christmas; many Christians prefer to keep them apart.

**Do not overload the line.** Five to eight symbols tell the story. A screen reader announces each emoji by name, so a thirty-emoji crèche becomes a long list of "ewe, ewe, ewe." The guide to [emoji accessibility and screen readers](/guides/emoji-accessibility-screen-readers/) explains what listeners hear, and is worth a look for parish accounts that serve older or visually impaired members.

**Put words first.** "Christ is born! 🌟" carries the meaning even if an emoji fails to display. A message made only of pictures can turn into boxes on an old phone.

**Expect designs to vary.** Apple, Google, Samsung, and Microsoft draw 🛖, 👼, and 🌟 differently, so the scene will not look identical for every reader. You can compare the official designs in the Unicode Consortium's [full emoji list](https://unicode.org/emoji/charts/full-emoji-list.html).

## Quick reference: the scene in one line

When you just need the right symbol fast, this rule of thumb covers almost every post: star on top, stable around, Child in the middle, family beside, animals at the edge, kings arriving last.

1. **Star:** 🌟 (or ⭐)
2. **Stable:** 🛖
3. **Child:** 👶
4. **Holy Family:** 👩 and 🧔, or 👪
5. **Animals:** 🐂 🫏 🐑
6. **Angels and shepherds:** 👼 🐑 🧑‍🌾
7. **Magi:** 👑 🐪 🎁, arriving at Epiphany

Put together, it reads 🌟🛖👩👶🧔🐂🫏🐑👼, followed in January by 👑👑👑🐪.
