The New Year's emojis people reach for most are fireworks 🎆, sparkles ✨, the party popper 🎉, clinking glasses 🥂, and the bottle with popping cork 🍾, with clocks and keycap numbers for the countdown. There is no emoji for 2027 itself, so the year is usually spelled with keycap digits (2️⃣0️⃣2️⃣7️⃣) or written in plain text next to a festive symbol. The rest of this guide covers what each emoji signals, how to build countdown and midnight messages, and greetings you can send to family, friends, and coworkers.

## The core New Year's emojis and what they say

Most New Year's emojis mean exactly what they show. The useful distinctions are about mood: some feel like a public celebration, some like a quiet toast, and some like a fresh start.

| Emoji | Official name | What it says in a New Year's message |
|---|---|---|
| 🎆 | Fireworks | Midnight, the big public display, "Happy New Year" in one symbol |
| 🎇 | Sparkler | A smaller, personal celebration; backyard or balcony fun |
| ✨ | Sparkles | Something new and shiny; good wishes; a softer festive accent |
| 🎉 | Party popper | Celebration, congratulations, "we made it" |
| 🎊 | Confetti ball | Party atmosphere; often paired with 🎉 |
| 🥳 | Partying face | "I am celebrating" from the sender's own point of view |
| 🥂 | Clinking glasses | A toast, "cheers to the new year" |
| 🍾 | Bottle with popping cork | Opening champagne at midnight; a big night out |
| 🪩 | Mirror ball | Dancing, a club or a themed party |
| 🕛 | Twelve o'clock | Midnight exactly |
| ⏳ | Hourglass not done | Time running out; "almost there" |
| 🌅 | Sunrise | The first sunrise of the year; a fresh start |
| 📝 | Memo | Resolutions, lists, plans |
| 🍀 | Four leaf clover | Luck for the year ahead |

The [fireworks](/emojis/fireworks/) emoji does the most work. On its own, sent on December 31 or January 1, it is understood as "Happy New Year" almost everywhere. The [sparkles](/emojis/sparkles/) emoji is the quieter alternative: it reads as warm and celebratory without the volume of a full fireworks show, which makes it a safe choice for work messages.

The [party popper](/emojis/party-popper/) and confetti ball are general celebration emojis that New Year's Eve borrows. They appear just as often for birthdays, promotions, and good news, so they lean on the date or the words around them to say "New Year." The [partying face](/emojis/partying-face/) is different in one way: it describes the sender. "🥳" alone tells people you are out celebrating, while "🎉" congratulates or celebrates with someone.

### Toasts and drinks

[Clinking glasses](/emojis/clinking-glasses/) is the standard toast. It looks like champagne flutes on most platforms, and "Cheers to 2027 🥂" is one of the most common New Year's captions. The bottle with popping cork adds energy: it says the cork is literally flying. If you are writing to someone who does not drink, or to a mixed audience at work, sparkles or fireworks carry the same festive meaning without the alcohol.

### Fireworks vs. sparkler

🎆 and 🎇 are separate emojis. Fireworks shows rockets bursting across a dark sky; sparkler shows a single handheld stick throwing sparks. Both are fine for New Year's Eve, but 🎆 suggests the citywide display at midnight and 🎇 feels like a small gathering at home. The two together, 🎆🎇, are a common opener for a midnight message. You can find both, along with the rest of the party symbols, in the [activities category](/categories/activities/).

## How to write 2027 with keycap emojis

There is no single emoji for 2027, or for any year. Unicode encodes symbols, not dates, so the year has to be built from parts. The usual method is four keycap digits in a row:

2️⃣0️⃣2️⃣7️⃣

To type it yourself:

1. Open the emoji keyboard on your phone or computer. If you are not sure where it is, [how to type emojis on any device](/guides/how-to-type-emojis-on-any-device/) covers the shortcuts for each system.
2. Search for "keycap" or for the digit itself, such as "2."
3. Tap 2️⃣, 0️⃣, 2️⃣, and 7️⃣ in order, with no spaces between them.
4. Copy the result into a note so you can paste it again on New Year's Eve.

The fastest route is simply to copy 2️⃣0️⃣2️⃣7️⃣ from this page, or tap the keycap row at the top and arrange the digits you need.

### Why keycap years can look odd

Each keycap emoji is a sequence of three characters: the ordinary digit, an invisible variation selector (U+FE0F) that asks for emoji style, and a combining enclosing keycap mark (U+20E3). When everything works, those three characters merge into one colored key. When something in the chain does not support the sequence, it falls apart. Common results:

- **A plain digit next to an empty box or outline.** The font or app has no drawing for the keycap mark. This is most likely on older operating systems and some desktop apps.
- **A plain black-and-white digit inside a thin square.** The variation selector was dropped, often by a form, a spreadsheet, or a social media scheduling tool, so the system falls back to text style.
- **Keys that look different on each phone.** Apple, Google, Samsung, and Microsoft all draw keycaps in their own style, and some designs are more legible than others. [iPhone vs. Android emojis](/guides/iphone-vs-android-emojis/) explains why.

If you see boxes on your own screen, [why emojis show as boxes](/guides/why-emojis-show-as-boxes/) walks through the fixes. When the message really matters, such as a company announcement or a card, write "2027" in plain text and put a single 🎆 after it. It will display correctly everywhere.

Screen readers are another reason to keep keycap years short. Each digit is announced separately, so a screen reader user may hear something close to "keycap two, keycap zero, keycap two, keycap seven" instead of "twenty twenty-seven." For one festive line that is fine; for a headline, plain text is kinder. [Emoji accessibility and screen readers](/guides/emoji-accessibility-screen-readers/) has more on this.

## Countdown and midnight messages

The countdown is where clock and number emojis earn their place. A few patterns that work:

- **The classic countdown:** "🔟 9️⃣ 8️⃣ 7️⃣ 6️⃣ 5️⃣ 4️⃣ 3️⃣ 2️⃣ 1️⃣ 🎆 HAPPY NEW YEAR"
- **The short version:** "3️⃣ 2️⃣ 1️⃣ 🎉"
- **The waiting-for-midnight post:** "⏳ almost there…" or "🕛 in 10 minutes 🥂"
- **The time-zone post:** "Already 2027 here 🎆 see you on the other side"
- **The late-night group chat:** "Who's still up? 🥳🍾"

The hourglass not done is good for anticipation earlier in the evening. The alarm clock works for reminders ("⏰ countdown starts at 11:59, be on the balcony") and, the next morning, for the less glamorous side of January 1.

### Mind the time zones

Midnight arrives at different times. Kiribati's Line Islands are among the first inhabited places to reach the new year, and American Samoa is among the last, roughly a full day later. If your family or team is spread out, a message sent at your midnight lands at a random hour for everyone else. Two simple fixes: send a "Happy New Year when it gets to you 🎆" message earlier in the day, or schedule messages so each person gets theirs near their own midnight.

These are written to be copied and adjusted. Two to four emojis per message is usually enough; more than that starts to bury the words.

### For coworkers and clients

Work messages need less sparkle, not none. One or two calm emojis is a good rule, and [emojis at work](/guides/emojis-at-work-etiquette/) covers how the norms shift between Slack, Teams, and email.

- "Happy New Year, team ✨ Thank you for all the work this year. Looking forward to 2027."
- "Wishing you and your family a happy and healthy new year 🎆"
- "Thank you for your partnership this year. Best wishes for 2027 ✨"
- "Happy New Year! 🎉 Quick reminder that the office reopens on January 4."

Avoid the champagne bottle, the partying face, and anything flirtatious in client messages. Sparkles and fireworks are the safest pair, and a plain-text "Happy New Year" with no emoji is never wrong for a formal email. ## Resolutions and fresh-start posts

Early January posts tend to swap party emojis for symbols of planning and change. The [memo](/emojis/memo/) is the default for a resolution list, the [seedling](/emojis/seedling/) suggests growth or a new beginning, and the [sunrise](/emojis/sunrise/) stands for the first morning of the year. The flexed biceps covers fitness goals, and the check mark button works for goals you plan to tick off.

Some formats that read naturally:

- **The numbered list:** "2027 goals 📝 1. Read 20 books 📚 2. Run a 10K 🏃 3. Call Mom more ❤️"
- **The one-word theme:** "My word for 2027: calm 🧘🌱"
- **The honest version:** "Resolution: drink water 💧 That's it. That's the list."
- **The look-back:** "2026: lessons learned 📚 2027: let's go 🌅"

If you want a symbol for "starting over," the counterclockwise arrows button 🔄 means refresh or restart, and the 🆕 button is a literal "new." For ideas on stringing these into small stories, see [emoji combos and ideas](/guides/emoji-combos-ideas/).

## Luck and wishes for the year ahead

Wishing someone luck is a large part of New Year's messages, and a handful of emojis carry it. The [four leaf clover](/emojis/four-leaf-clover/) is the clearest luck symbol in Western usage. [Crossed fingers](/emojis/crossed-fingers/) means "hoping for the best," which suits a wish like "🤞 this is our year." The shooting star 🌠, filed with the sky and weather symbols in [travel and places](/categories/travel-and-places/), suggests making a wish, and folded hands adds a note of gratitude or prayer, depending on the reader.

Some luck emojis are more playful. The crystal ball works for "predictions for 2027 🔮" posts, and the money bag 💰 is a common half-joking wish for a better-paid year. The nazar amulet 🧿, a blue eye charm against bad luck from Turkish and wider Mediterranean and Middle Eastern tradition, shows up in New Year's wishes from people who grew up with it.

## New Year traditions and their emojis around the world

January 1 is not the only new year, and many places mark it with their own customs. These are traditions that have clear emoji matches. [Emojis across cultures](/guides/emojis-across-cultures/) covers more of the ways meaning shifts between countries.

| Tradition | Where | Emojis people use |
|---|---|---|
| Twelve grapes at midnight, one per chime | Spain and many Spanish-speaking countries | 🍇 🕛 🔔 |
| Good-luck pigs and four leaf clovers as gifts | Germany, Austria, Switzerland | 🐖 🍀 |
| Pine decorations at the door; temple bells rung at midnight | Japan | 🎍 🔔 🌅 |
| Times Square ball drop | New York, United States | 🗽 🎆 🕛 |
| Red envelopes, lanterns, firecrackers | Lunar New Year, across East and Southeast Asia | 🧧 🏮 🧨 |

In Spain, eating twelve [grapes](/emojis/grapes/) in time with the twelve chimes of midnight is a well-known custom, so 🍇 paired with the [flag of Spain](/emojis/flag-spain/) is an instant reference there. In Japan, the New Year period is the most important holiday of the year; the 🎍 pine decoration emoji is based on the kadomatsu placed at entrances, and people send greetings with the Japanese flag or the first sunrise 🌅.

### Lunar New Year is a different date

Lunar New Year, also called Chinese New Year or Spring Festival, follows the lunisolar calendar and falls between late January and mid-February. In 2027 it begins on February 6, opening the Year of the Goat (sometimes translated as Sheep). Its emojis are distinct: the red envelope 🧧 for the money gifts given to children and younger relatives, the red paper lantern 🏮, the firecracker 🧨, and the year's zodiac animal, here 🐐 or 🐑. Sending 🧧 on December 31 would be out of place; save it for February.

Other calendars have their own new years too, such as Rosh Hashanah in the Jewish calendar (often marked with 🍎 and 🍯 for apples and honey) and Nowruz, the Persian New Year at the March equinox. If you are greeting someone for one of these, use their holiday's name and symbols rather than reusing a December 31 fireworks message.
