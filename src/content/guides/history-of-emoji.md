Emoji started as a competitive feature on Japanese mobile phones in the late 1990s, spent a decade as an incompatible mess of carrier-specific symbols, and became a worldwide writing tool after the Unicode Consortium standardized them in October 2010. The familiar origin story credits Shigetaka Kurita and NTT DoCoMo in 1999, but the first phone emoji set shipped two years earlier, and the idea of adding pictures to text is older still.

This guide follows the timeline in order: what came before emoji, the Japanese carrier era, how Unicode got involved, and how the set has changed since. If you want the mechanics of how a new emoji is chosen today, that has its own guide: [how new emojis get approved](/guides/how-new-emojis-get-approved/).

## Before emoji: emoticons, kaomoji and dingbats

People were decorating text with pictures long before phones could draw them.

On September 19, 1982, computer scientist Scott Fahlman posted a message to a Carnegie Mellon University bulletin board proposing that jokes be marked with `:-)` and serious posts with `:-(`. Earlier typographic faces exist in print, but Fahlman's post is the documented starting point of the sideways smiley as an online convention. These are **emoticons**: faces built from punctuation that is already on the keyboard.

Japanese internet users developed their own style in the 1980s, usually called **kaomoji** ("face characters"). Kaomoji such as `(^_^)` and `(T_T)` are read upright rather than sideways and put the emotion in the eyes instead of the mouth. That habit carried over into emoji design, which is one reason the early Japanese sets contained so many faces distinguished mainly by eye shape. It also explains details that still puzzle Western users, such as the snot bubble on the [sleepy face](/emojis/sleepy-face/), a manga convention for someone dozing off.

A third ancestor is the symbol font. Fonts such as Zapf Dingbats (1978) and Microsoft's Wingdings (1990) mapped small pictures to ordinary keys: scissors, pointing hands, telephones, check marks. They were not emoji, because the underlying text was still a plain letter that only looked like a picture in that one font. Decades later, many Wingdings and Webdings symbols were added to Unicode and some became emoji.

The quick distinction:

- **Emoticon**: ordinary characters arranged to look like a face. `:-)` is three characters.
- **Kaomoji**: the upright Japanese style of emoticon, often using characters from several scripts.
- **Dingbat**: a picture stored in a font in place of a letter. Change the font and the picture disappears.
- **Emoji**: a pictograph with its own code point. It remains the same character no matter which font draws it.

## The pager heart and the 1997 J-Phone set

In mid-1990s Japan, pagers were hugely popular with teenagers, and NTT DoCoMo's Pocket Bell line included a heart symbol that could be sent in a message. Kurita has said in interviews that the popularity of that single heart, and the complaints when a later business-oriented pager dropped it, convinced him that people wanted a way to add feeling to short digital messages.

The first known emoji set on a mobile phone came from DoCoMo's competitor. In November 1997, J-Phone (a carrier that later became part of Vodafone Japan and then SoftBank) released the SkyWalker DP-211SW, a handset with 90 built-in pictographs. The set was black and white and included numbers, weather, moon phases, hearts, hand gestures, a few faces and, notably, a [pile of poo](/emojis/pile-of-poo/). The phone was expensive and did not sell well, so the set had little influence at the time and was largely forgotten until emoji historians documented it in 2019.

Researchers have since pointed to even earlier pictographic character sets on Japanese electronic organizers and word processors from the late 1980s and early 1990s. Where exactly to draw the line for "first emoji" is a matter of definition. What is clear is that the idea was circulating in Japanese consumer electronics well before 1999.

## 1999: Shigetaka Kurita and the i-mode set

NTT DoCoMo launched i-mode, an early mobile internet service, in February 1999. Kurita, then a young member of the i-mode team, designed a set of 176 pictographs for it. Each was drawn on a 12 by 12 pixel grid, which leaves 144 dots to suggest a face, a train or a cocktail glass.

Kurita's goal was practical. i-mode screens were tiny and messages were limited in length, so a single character that could say "sunny," "restaurant" or "I'm joking" saved space and softened curt text. He has cited manga symbols, weather-forecast pictograms, street signs and Chinese characters as sources. The original 176 leaned heavily toward information: weather, traffic, technology, zodiac signs, phases of the moon. Only a handful were faces, and several were hearts, including a plain [red heart](/emojis/red-heart/) and a [broken heart](/emojis/broken-heart/).

The set was a commercial success, and it is the reason emoji became a standard feature of Japanese phones. In 2016 the Museum of Modern Art in New York added the original 176 DoCoMo emoji to its collection, which cemented Kurita's set as the canonical starting point in popular accounts.

## The carrier wars: three sets that could not talk to each other

DoCoMo's rivals responded with their own emoji. By the early 2000s Japan had three major sets, from DoCoMo, KDDI (under its au brand) and J-Phone/Vodafone (later SoftBank). Each carrier:

1. chose its own list of pictures,
2. drew them in its own style, with later sets adding color and even animation, and
3. stored them at its own private positions in its own text encoding.

The result was that an emoji sent from one carrier to another often arrived as a blank, a different picture or a placeholder symbol. Carriers eventually built conversion tables to translate between sets at the network level, but the mappings were imperfect because the sets did not contain the same pictures.

Several quirks of today's emoji keyboard are leftovers of this period. The large number of Japanese-specific symbols, such as the squared kanji buttons, the [moai](/emojis/moai/) statue and specific foods and festival objects, come straight from the carrier sets. So do near-duplicates: when two carriers had slightly different hearts or moons, Unicode later had to keep both so that existing Japanese text could be converted without loss. And the carriers included just ten national flags, which influenced [how flag emojis were eventually encoded](/guides/flag-emojis-explained/).

## How emoji got into Unicode

Unicode is the standard that assigns a number, called a code point, to every character in every writing system so that text survives the trip between devices. In the mid-2000s emoji were not in it.

That became a problem for companies entering the Japanese market. Google wanted Gmail to exchange emoji with Japanese phone mail, and Apple needed emoji for the iPhone's launch with SoftBank. Engineers at Google began mapping the three carrier sets around 2006 and 2007, and a formal proposal to encode emoji in Unicode followed, with Apple engineers joining as co-authors. Mark Davis, a Unicode co-founder who worked at Google, was closely involved.

The proposal was debated for several years. One recurring question was whether pictures of hamburgers belonged in a character standard at all. The winning argument was compatibility: these characters were already being exchanged by tens of millions of Japanese users, and Unicode's job is to represent text that exists.

Meanwhile, Apple shipped an emoji keyboard in iPhone OS 2.2 in November 2008, officially for Japan only. Users in other countries discovered they could enable it with third-party apps, which gave many people outside Japan their first taste of emoji.

**Unicode 6.0**, released in October 2010, is the turning point. It added more than 700 emoji characters drawn from the Japanese carrier sets, plus the 26 regional indicator symbols used to build flags. From then on, a [face with tears of joy](/emojis/face-with-tears-of-joy/) was the same character everywhere, even though each company still drew it differently. Why those drawings differ, and what that does to meaning, is covered in [iPhone vs Android emojis](/guides/iphone-vs-android-emojis/).

## 2011 to 2015: emoji go global

With a standard in place, the platforms moved quickly.

- **2011**: Apple made the emoji keyboard available to all users in iOS 5 and added a color emoji font to Mac OS X Lion.
- **2012**: Windows 8 added emoji support, initially in black and white.
- **2013**: Android gained native color emoji. Emojipedia, the reference site founded by Jeremy Burge, launched the same year.
- **2014**: Unicode 7.0 added roughly 250 emoji, many of them converted from Wingdings and Webdings, including the [middle finger](/emojis/middle-finger/). The first World Emoji Day was held on July 17, the date shown on Apple's calendar emoji.
- **2015**: Unicode 8.0 introduced five skin tone modifiers based on the Fitzpatrick scale, along with long-requested additions such as the taco and the [unicorn](/emojis/unicorn/). Oxford Dictionaries named the face with tears of joy emoji its Word of the Year.

The skin tone change mattered beyond its immediate purpose. It was the first time an emoji's appearance was controlled by a second character following the first, and it set the pattern for nearly everything that came later. The details are in [emoji skin tones explained](/guides/emoji-skin-tones-explained/).

## 2016 onward: representation, sequences and redesigns

Once emoji were everywhere, the questions shifted from "can I send this?" to "who and what is missing?"

### Gender and people

In 2016, professions such as doctor, firefighter and scientist were added in both female and male versions. They were built as **zero width joiner (ZWJ) sequences**: an existing person emoji, an invisible joining character, and an object emoji that together display as one picture. This technique meant new people emoji no longer needed new code points. Later releases added gender-neutral versions of most people emoji, hair options (red, curly, white, bald) in 2018, and mixed skin tones for couples starting in 2019.

### Accessibility and identity

The 2019 release added a group of accessibility emoji proposed by Apple, including wheelchairs, a guide dog, a probing cane and an ear with a hearing aid. The [rainbow flag](/emojis/rainbow-flag/) arrived in 2016 and the [transgender flag](/emojis/transgender-flag/) in 2020, both built as sequences.

### Redesigns that changed meaning

Because each company draws its own images, a vendor can change what an emoji says without Unicode changing anything. The best-known case is the pistol. In 2016 Apple redrew its realistic revolver as a green water gun, and by 2018 most other major vendors had followed. The character is now officially named [water pistol](/emojis/water-pistol/). Vendors have also converged over time on details that used to cause confusion, such as the expression on the [grimacing face](/emojis/grimacing-face/).

### Smaller, slower releases

The early 2020s releases added emotionally specific faces that quickly became popular, including [melting face](/emojis/melting-face/), [face holding back tears](/emojis/face-holding-back-tears/) and [shaking face](/emojis/shaking-face/), plus the long-requested plain [pink heart](/emojis/pink-heart/). At the same time, the number of brand-new characters per year fell sharply. The 2024 release contained only a handful of new concepts, among them the [face with bags under eyes](/emojis/face-with-bags-under-eyes/). The 2025 release is covered in [new emojis in 2026](/guides/new-emojis-2026/).

Part of the reason is that the people who maintain the standard now prefer emoji that can carry several meanings over narrowly literal objects. Another is that platforms have built alternatives that do not need standardization, such as stickers, Google's [Emoji Kitchen](/guides/emoji-kitchen-guide/) mashups and AI-generated custom emoji on recent iPhones. Those are images, not text characters, so they do not travel between apps the way real emoji do.

## How the meaning of emoji drifted from the originals

Kurita's set was mostly signage. What people actually did with emoji over the next 25 years was closer to tone of voice. The hearts multiplied into a color-coded vocabulary, described in the [heart emoji colors guide](/guides/heart-emoji-colors-meaning/). The [skull](/emojis/skull/) came to mean laughter for younger users, as explained in [Gen Z emoji slang](/guides/gen-z-emoji-slang/). The [folded hands](/emojis/folded-hands/), a Japanese gesture for please and thank you, became a prayer in much of the West.

None of that was designed. The Japanese origins explain why certain emoji exist; usage explains what they mean now. For current meanings, start with [emoji meanings in texting](/guides/emoji-meanings-in-texting/), and for regional differences see [emojis across cultures](/guides/emojis-across-cultures/).
