Emojis can help marketing copy in two ways: they catch the eye in a crowded inbox or notification tray, and they set a tone that plain text takes more words to establish. They hurt when they replace the actual message, clash with the brand or the moment, break on the recipient's device, or carry a second meaning the writer did not know about. There is no universal rule that emojis raise or lower response, so the practical approach is to learn the constraints of each channel, pick emojis that add something specific, and test.

## What emojis can and cannot do for a message

It helps to be precise about the job you are giving the emoji.

**Things an emoji does well**

- Creates a visual break in a list of text-only subject lines or notifications
- Signals a category at a glance: a [wrapped gift](/emojis/wrapped-gift/) for a gift guide, an [alarm clock](/emojis/alarm-clock/) for a deadline, a [bell](/emojis/bell/) for a reminder
- Softens or warms a short message that would otherwise sound curt
- Marks tone: celebration, humor, urgency

**Things an emoji does not do**

- Fix a vague offer. "Something big is coming 👀" is still vague.
- Substitute for the benefit. "🔥🔥🔥" says nothing about what is on sale.
- Work the same way forever. If every email from you starts with a [fire](/emojis/fire/) emoji, it stops being a signal.
- Make a serious message friendlier. On a price increase, an outage notice or an apology, an emoji reads as flippant.

A useful test: delete the emoji and read the line again. If the line is now unclear, the copy was leaning on the emoji and needs rewriting. If the line is fine and the emoji added tone or scannability, keep it.

## Email subject lines and preheaders

Email is the channel with the most technical variation, because you do not control the software that displays your message.

### How subject line emojis render

Each mail client draws emojis with whatever font or image set it has. Apple Mail uses Apple's artwork, Gmail on the web uses Google's designs, and Outlook on Windows uses Microsoft's emoji font. The same [party popper](/emojis/party-popper/) therefore looks different to different subscribers, and older desktop software may show a flat monochrome symbol or an empty box. Differences between vendors are covered in [iPhone vs Android emojis](/guides/iphone-vs-android-emojis/).

Practical consequences:

- **Stick to established emojis.** Anything approved in the last couple of years will be missing on a share of devices. The symptoms and causes are explained in [why emojis show as boxes](/guides/why-emojis-show-as-boxes/).
- **Avoid emojis whose designs diverge.** Faces with subtle expressions vary most. Objects and symbols vary least.
- **Send yourself tests** on at least an iPhone, an Android phone, Gmail on the web and desktop Outlook before a large send.

### Placement and length

Mobile inboxes cut subject lines short, and the exact cutoff varies by app, device and font size. Put the words that matter first. An emoji at the very start takes one of your most visible positions; that is worthwhile when the emoji is a category signal and wasteful when it is decoration. An emoji at the end may be cut off, which is harmless if it was only tone.

The preheader, the gray preview text after the subject, is a lower-risk place to experiment. An emoji there adds color without changing the subject line your subscribers recognize.

Do not put emojis in the sender name. The "from" field is how people decide whether a message is really from you, and decorating it is a habit associated with junk mail.

### Deliverability

Mailbox providers do not publish their filtering rules, but the factors they describe publicly are sender authentication, reputation, complaint rates and engagement. An emoji on its own is not known to be a spam trigger. What does cause trouble is resembling spam in aggregate: stacked [money bag](/emojis/money-bag/) and warning symbols, all capitals, false urgency. If your subject line would look at home in a junk folder, the emoji is the least of its problems.

## Push notifications and SMS

### Push

A push notification is a few dozen visible characters on a lock screen, and the emoji shares that space with the offer. One emoji, placed where it will not push key words out of view, is the norm. Because notifications are drawn by the phone's operating system, the too-new-emoji problem applies directly: a customer on a three-year-old Android phone will see a box where your newest emoji should be.

Push is also the most interruptive channel. Emojis that imply alarm, such as sirens or a [warning](/emojis/warning/) sign, should be reserved for things that are urgent from the customer's point of view: a delivery arriving, a fraud alert, a booking about to lapse. Using them for a routine sale teaches people to ignore, or disable, your notifications.

### SMS

Text messages have a cost trap that surprises many teams. A standard SMS in the basic GSM alphabet holds 160 characters. Emojis are not in that alphabet, so including even one switches the whole message to a Unicode encoding that holds 70 characters per segment, and fewer when the message is split into several linked parts. A 150-character promotion that was one billable segment becomes three as soon as you add a [sparkles](/emojis/sparkles/) emoji.

That does not make emojis wrong for SMS; it makes them a budget decision. Check your provider's segment counter with the emoji in place. Also watch for characters that look ordinary but force the same switch, such as curly quotes pasted from a document.

| Channel | Main constraint | Sensible default |
|---|---|---|
| Email subject line | Rendering varies by client; mobile truncation | One emoji, established design, words first |
| Email preheader | Truncation | Good place to test |
| Push notification | Very short; drawn by the phone's OS | One emoji, never a brand-new one |
| SMS | Any emoji cuts a segment from 160 to 70 characters | Use only if the segment count still works |
| Social caption | Platform slang and audience norms | A few, each with a purpose |
| Paid search ads | Platform editorial policy | Check the policy; Google Ads has long restricted emojis in ad text |
| Web page titles | Search engines decide what to display | Do not rely on them appearing |

## Social captions, comments and replies

Social platforms are where emojis are most at home and where the conventions change fastest.

**Captions.** Emojis can break a long caption into scannable parts, mark a call to action, or set mood at the end. Using them as bullet points at the start of every line is common, but it is hard on screen reader users, who hear each emoji's full name before every item. Real line breaks and short sentences do the same job.

**Character budgets.** X counts every emoji as two characters toward its limit, regardless of how the emoji is built internally. Other platforms have their own counting rules, so check the counter, not your own estimate.

**Comments and replies.** This is where brands most often try on platform slang, such as the [skull](/emojis/skull/) for "that's hilarious" or the [loudly crying face](/emojis/loudly-crying-face/) for laughter. It can work for brands whose community managers actually live in that culture. It fails when the usage is a year stale or when a serious customer complaint gets a jokey reply. The current conventions are in the guides to [Instagram emoji meanings](/guides/instagram-emoji-meanings/), [TikTok emoji meanings](/guides/tiktok-emoji-meanings/) and [Gen Z emoji slang](/guides/gen-z-emoji-slang/).

**Campaign mechanics.** Emojis have occasionally been the campaign itself. Domino's let customers in the United States order by tweeting a pizza emoji in 2015, Chevrolet issued a press release written in emojis the same year, and WWF ran a fundraising campaign built around emojis of endangered animals. Oxford Dictionaries naming the [face with tears of joy](/emojis/face-with-tears-of-joy/) its Word of the Year for 2015 marked the peak of that novelty. A decade on, an emoji stunt is no longer news by itself; it needs a real idea behind it. World Emoji Day on July 17, the date shown on the calendar emoji on many platforms, remains a reliable hook for light content.

## Risks: double meanings, rendering, culture and accessibility

### Double meanings

Several ordinary-looking emojis have slang meanings that a general audience knows well.

- The [eggplant](/emojis/eggplant/) and [peach](/emojis/peach/) are sexual innuendo. A grocery brand can sometimes use them literally, with care; almost no one else can.
- [Sweat droplets](/emojis/sweat-droplets/) reads as sexual in many contexts, which matters for fitness and beverage brands.
- The [clown face](/emojis/clown-face/) is an insult aimed at someone who has made a fool of themselves.
- Snowflake, leaf, pill and similar emojis are used as drug references in some communities.
- The plain [slightly smiling face](/emojis/slightly-smiling-face/) reads as passive-aggressive to many younger readers.

Look up any emoji you are not sure about before it goes into a send to a large list. A mistake in a personal text is a laugh; in a campaign it is a screenshot.

### Designs change under you

Vendors redraw emojis. The best-known case is the pistol, which Apple changed to a [water pistol](/emojis/water-pistol/) in 2016, with other major vendors following in 2018. For a period, the same character was a toy on one phone and a realistic handgun on another. If your message depends on exactly how an emoji looks, it depends on something you do not control.

### Culture and region

A gesture that is positive at home can be rude elsewhere. The [thumbs up](/emojis/thumbs-up/) and OK hand are the standard examples, and color symbolism differs too. If a campaign crosses borders, have someone local review it; the specifics are in [emojis across cultures](/guides/emojis-across-cultures/). For skin-toned gestures, the long-standing brand convention is the default yellow, as explained in [emoji skin tones](/guides/emoji-skin-tones-explained/).

### Accessibility

Screen readers speak each emoji's name aloud. A subject line that begins with three emojis makes a blind subscriber listen to three descriptions before the first word of your offer. Keep emojis few, put them after the words, and never let one carry information, such as a discount or a date, that is not also written out. The full guidance is in [emoji accessibility and screen readers](/guides/emoji-accessibility-screen-readers/).

### Legal and policy points

This is general information, not legal advice.

- **Text versus artwork.** Using emoji characters in text is ordinary writing. The images are another matter: Apple's, Samsung's and other vendors' emoji designs are their own artwork. For print, packaging, video and merchandise, use an openly licensed set such as Google's Noto Emoji, Twemoji or OpenMoji, and follow the license terms.
- **Emojis can carry legal weight.** In a widely reported 2023 decision, a court in Saskatchewan, Canada, found that a thumbs-up emoji sent in reply to a contract had signaled acceptance. Customer-facing teams should not use approving emojis loosely in negotiations or disputes.
- **Ad platform rules.** Each ad platform sets its own editorial policy on symbols. Read the current policy before building creative around an emoji.
- **Disclosures.** An emoji is not a substitute for clearly worded sponsorship or advertising disclosure.

## Fitting emojis to your brand voice

Whether to use emojis at all is a brand decision before it is a tactical one.

1. **Read what customers send you.** If support tickets and replies are full of emojis, your audience is comfortable with them. If they are formal, follow suit.
2. **Define a small house set.** Five to ten emojis that fit the brand, with a note on what each is for, keeps a team consistent and avoids the random-emoji look.
3. **Set the no-go situations.** Outages, security incidents, recalls, bereavement, layoffs, legal notices, payment failures and anything involving health or money trouble get no emojis.
4. **Decide who can improvise.** Reactive social replies need latitude; scheduled email does not.
5. **Write it down.** Put the rules in the style guide beside capitalization and tone, the same way you would document internal norms for [emojis at work](/guides/emojis-at-work-etiquette/).

Seasonal sends are the easiest place to start, because the emoji vocabulary is obvious and expected. A [Christmas tree](/emojis/christmas-tree/) in December surprises nobody; see [Christmas emoji meanings](/guides/christmas-emojis-meanings/) for options beyond the obvious.

## How to test emojis on your own audience

Because results differ by audience, your own test is worth more than any published benchmark.

1. **Change one thing.** Same subject line, same send time, same segment split at random. Version A has the emoji; version B does not.
2. **Pick the right metric.** Opens are inflated and distorted by Apple Mail Privacy Protection, which loads messages in the background. Use clicks, conversions or revenue per recipient. For push, use the action you wanted, not just notification opens.
3. **Watch the negative signals.** Compare unsubscribes, spam complaints and push opt-outs between versions. A variant that wins on clicks and loses on opt-outs is not a win.
4. **Use enough volume.** Small differences on small lists are noise. If your tool reports statistical confidence, wait for it; if the list is small, repeat the test across several sends before deciding.
5. **Test position and choice separately.** Once you know whether an emoji helps at all, test start versus end, then one emoji against another.
6. **Retest periodically.** Novelty wears off. An emoji that worked in spring may be invisible by autumn if you used it every week.

## Pre-send checklist

- The line is clear and complete with the emoji removed.
- One or two emojis at most in a subject line or push notification.
- Each emoji is at least a couple of years old and renders on a test iPhone, Android phone and desktop mail client.
- No emoji with a known slang, sexual or drug meaning unless that is deliberate and reviewed.
- No emoji in the sender name.
- SMS segment count checked with the emoji included.
- Tone matches the moment: nothing playful on bad news.
- Gestures and colors reviewed for every market the message reaches.
- Key information is in words, with emojis after the words for screen reader users.
- Any emoji artwork used outside plain text comes from a properly licensed set.
- The test plan measures clicks or conversions and tracks opt-outs.
